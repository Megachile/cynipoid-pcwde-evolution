# Abstract

Horizontal gene transfer from bacteria can enable dramatic shifts in animal resource use by delivering enzymes already adapted for a biochemical function. One such shift occurred in cynipoid wasps, one of the few lineages of the primarily entomophagous apocritan wasps to have transitioned to herbivory. Plant cell walls pose particular digestive challenges for apocritan larvae because their blind midgut prevents defecation of indigestible fiber. Building on prior work identifying three cynipoid plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (PCWDEs) as bacterially derived, we use a comparative genomic approach to evaluate the contribution of these enzymes to the evolution of herbivory in Cynipoidea. Analysis of 49 genomes, including a new genome for *Euceroptres primus*, supports independent transfers of three PCWDEs near the crown of Cynipoidea, approximately 159–195 million years ago. All three genes are retained in herbivorous, gall-associated lineages in Cynipidae and Diplolepididae, but are broadly absent in the entomophagous family Figitidae. Within oak gall wasps, PCWDEs are highly expressed in larval midguts, where enzymes likely aid digestion by solubilizing cell wall polymers. Duplications produced additional paralogs expressed in larval salivary glands and adult venom glands, indicating secondary recruitment into plant interactions potentially implicated in gall induction. Finally, we recover an unreported fourth bacterial transfer of a complex multidomain protein expressed in oak gall wasps as a B-lectin domain. This evidence demonstrates that bacterial gene transfer supplied key molecular tools enabling herbivory and helped establish the ecological context in which cynipoid gall induction evolved.

# Introduction

Plants are one of the most abundant biological resources on Earth, but their tissues pose substantial nutritional and defensive challenges to consumers (Futuyma & Agrawal 2009; Behmer 2009; Mithöfer & Boland 2012). They are often low in nutrient density, high in fiber, and can contain toxic defensive compounds. In spite of this, herbivory has evolved repeatedly in animals, demonstrating the strong ecological opportunity offered by this abundant resource (Futuyma & Agrawal 2009). Herbivorous insects have developed a wide variety of solutions to these problems, including microbial symbioses, detoxification mechanisms, and behavioral specialization on particular plant tissues (Hansen & Moran 2014; Després et al. 2007; Tooker & Giron 2020). 

Among insects, microbial associations are a common solution to the challenges of herbivory. Digestive endosymbionts can aid their hosts (e.g., termites, aphids, beetles) by detoxifying defensive compounds, synthesizing necessary nutrients, or degrading otherwise inaccessible plant substrates (Engel and Moran 2013; Kucuk et al. 2023). In some cases, bacterial functions have been acquired permanently by a eukaryotic genome through horizontal gene transfer. In some cases, these genes originate from obligate reproductive or digestive endosymbionts, although in many cases no extant symbiont can be identified (Wybouw et al. 2016). A large proportion of such bacterial transfers to insects involve plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (PCWDEs), including bacterial mannanases, xylanases, pectinases, and cellulase-like enzymes that help insects process otherwise indigestible plant polysaccharides (Acuña et al. 2012; Pauchet and Heckel, 2013; Kirsch et al. 2014; Shelomi et al. 2014; Wybouw et al. 2016). Lineage-scale studies of stick insects and cerambycid beetles have shown that these transfers have preceded radiations into herbivorous niches and that transferred genes subsequently diversified through duplication, regulatory divergence, or changes in substrate specificity (Shelomi et al. 2016; Shin et al. 2022).

Hymenoptera (bees, ants, and wasps) have an interesting evolutionary history with respect to herbivory. While extant members of the group are primarily entomophagous, ancestral hymenopterans were likely herbivores resembling modern-day sawflies (Blaimer et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025a), which pass undigested plant material as frass. The group transitioned to entomophagous parasitoidism more than 200 million years ago near the origin of the suborder Apocrita (Zhang et al. 2025a). Apocritan larvae are characterized by a blind midgut, which is not connected to the hindgut. Food enters the midgut, where enzymatic digestion occurs. While soluble nutrients are absorbed through the gut lining, indigestible residues accumulate in the midgut until they are expelled as a meconium before pupation (Quicke 1997; Bağrıaçık 2020). The blind midgut presents an obstacle to any potential transition back to consuming plants. Without the capacity to defecate, insoluble cell wall polymers ingested by chewing plant tissue would accumulate in the midgut, limiting total intake volume and potentially preventing adequate nutrition for pupation. Several transitions back to herbivory have occurred in Apocrita, but none of them include the direct leaf feeding seen in sawflies (Tooker and Giron 2020). 

While some Apocritan herbivores mitigate digestive challenges by feeding on existing plant resources with high nutrient density like nectar and pollen (bees) or seeds (seed-feeding ants), gall wasps elicit novel resources. Gall wasps co-opt the development of their host plants to induce and control development of a parasite-specific organ called a gall. Gall wasp larvae then feed on specialized nutritive tissue lining a central larval chamber (Bronner 1992; Brooks and Shorthouse 1998; Stone et al. 2002; Stone and Schönrogge 2003). Larvae can feed for months, and during this time they remain confined to their larval chamber and cannot expel indigestible material. Nutritive cells are enriched in nutrients and largely lack secondary cell walls (Bronner 1992; Brooks and Shorthouse 1998), but their cell walls still contain cellulose and pectin (Cosgrove 2005). Cleavage of these polysaccharides could reduce accumulation of indigestible material in the larval midgut (Hearn et al. 2019).

Digestive endosymbionts are not unknown in Apocrita, but are primarily facultative, and have not been documented in Cynipoidea (Brucker and Bordenstein 2012). However, horizontally transferred PCWDEs have been observed in cynipid wasps (Cambier et al. 2019; Hearn et al. 2019). Cynipid gall-forming wasps comprise one of the most structurally complex and phenotypically variable groups of gall inducers. Cynipids are diverse and distributed worldwide, with over 1,400 described species (Ronquist et al. 2015; Buffington et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2026). All known members of the family are gall inhabitants, but within that context, multiple feeding strategies are found, including multiple independently-evolved lineages of gall inducers, multiple lineages of herbivorous gall inquilines, and some lineages that may be at least partly entomophagous (Blair 1949). The largest tribe, Cynipini, is entirely composed of gall inducers, contains more than 1,000 described species, and is primarily associated with oaks (family Fagaceae). Many Cynipini with known life cycles alternate between a sexual generation, containing males and females, and an asexual generation, containing only females. Each generation typically induces a distinct gall and has a distinct adult female morphology. Hearn et al. (2019) reported expression of three classes of PCWDEs in sexual generation feeding larvae of *Biorhiza pallida*: rhamnogalacturonan lyase, pectin/pectate lyase, and cellulase. Subsequent work reported expression of all three enzymes across larval development in *Diplolepis rosae* as well (Mozhaitseva et al. 2023). A preliminary survey of available genomes suggested that PCWDE acquisition occurred early in the evolutionary history of cynipoids and that copy numbers of each gene vary across lineages (Hearn et al. 2019). 

As putative digestive enzymes, the primary expected context for insect expression of PCWDEs would be in the larval gut. Surprisingly, however, high expression of an enzyme in Cynipini labeled β-1,4-endoglucanase, indicating the potential capacity to degrade cellulose, was first observed in the adult female venom gland of *Biorhiza pallida* (Cambier et al. 2019). This gene expression is associated with direct biochemical evidence of both cellulolytic and pectinolytic activity on *B. pallida* eggs (Bronner 1973). Such evidence suggests that cynipid PCWDEs have important host-interaction functions beyond larval digestion. In plants, β-1,4-glucanases and pectate lyase-like enzymes are expressed in response to tissue wounding (Ikeuchi et al. 2022\) and can facilitate tissue remodeling  (Notaguchi et al. 2020; Ye et al. 2025\) and callus formation (Kawakatsu et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2024). The functional applications of PCWDEs in Cynipini may be more diverse than direct digestion alone. 

The transfer, retention, expression, and expansion of PCWDEs indicate that they play an important role in cynipoid wasp biology, possibly facilitating both herbivory and gall induction. To evaluate this history, we tested two sets of predictions. If each PCWDE family derives from a single ancient bacterial transfer, cynipoid copies should form a monophyletic lineage broadly consistent with known patterns of descent, and many genes should share chromosome context or intron positions. Ecologically, if these genes contributed to the transition to herbivory, their retention and expansion should be concentrated in herbivorous, gall-associated lineages, catalytic residues should be conserved, and expression should be concentrated in tissues associated with digestion or plant interaction. We examine 48 cynipoid genomes, including a new chromosome-scale assembly for *Euceroptres primus*, an early-diverging member of the entomophagous family Figitidae and one of the few sampled figitids still associated with galls, and one outgroup, the platygastroid wasp *Telenomus remus*. We also analyze RNA-seq data from whole larvae of 14 cynipid species, larval tissues of two Cynipini species, and adult tissues of seven Cynipini species. Finally, we identify a previously undescribed bacterially derived gene family, originally acquired as a multidomain protein containing endo-β-1,6-glucanase and B-lectin domains but retained primarily as a non-catalytic B-lectin. Together, these analyses document the genomic domestication of bacterial carbohydrate-associated genes and clarify how an ancestrally entomophagous lineage acquired a molecular toolkit for consuming and manipulating plant tissue. 

# Results

## Insect genome and transcriptome evidence was gathered from multiple sources

To analyze the history of bacterially transferred genes in Cynipoidea, we curated a core analytic set of 49 annotated genomes, including the Platygastroid outgroup T. remus, 3 Diplolepididae (rose and maple gall inducers), 11 Figitidae (entomophagous parasitoids), and 34 Cynipidae (herb, bramble, and oak gall inducing and inquiline wasps) (Table S1). The genome of *Cecinothofagus ibarrai* was available but fell below the quality threshold (15.3% genome BUSCO completeness), so the basal cynipoid family Paraulacidae is not represented. Because preliminary work showed that the figitid species *Euceroptres primus* was the only sampled figitid to retain PCWDEs, we generated a new chromosome-scale genome assembly for this species to resolve the chromosomal context of each gene. Full genome assembly methods and details are available in Supplement 1\. 

Genomic data were supplemented with de novo protein predictions assembled from RNA-seq reads from a variety of sample types, from whole larvae or adults to dissected tissues to whole galls containing small larvae. Reads from four species were downloaded from previously published datasets, while an additional ten were generated from new sequencing work. Full sampling information and methodology appear in Supplement 2\. Table S1 lists genome, de novo assembly, and RNA-seq expression availability per species. 

## Genes belong to known CAZyme subfamilies 

Cynipoid members of each putative PCWDE family were screened against the dbCAN and CAZyme3D databases to match them to known catalytic classes and infer plausible enzyme activity. Sequences and structures were consistently assigned to three CAZy subfamilies: (1) GH5\_2, (2) PL1\_8, or (3) PL4\_4. 

(1) Genes annotated as “Endoglucanase z-like” were assigned to CAZy subfamily GH5\_2. This subfamily contains EC 3.2.1.4 cellulase activity. Its known substrates are β-1,4 glucan polymers including cellulose, and it generates short soluble cellodextrin β-glucans as byproducts via endo-hydrolysis of β-1,4 bonds. To determine whether these short cellodextrins could be further broken down into metabolically available glucose, we also searched for enzymes known to have catalytic activity targeting cellodextrins. GH1\_e72, a natively eukaryotic enzyme with EC 3.2.1.21 β-glucosidase activity, was identified as a candidate degrader of β-D-glucans, though cellodextrin is not specified as a substrate. (2) Genes annotated as “Pectin lyase” were assigned to PL1\_8, which is listed to include EC 4.2.2.10 pectin lyase activity. This enzyme cleaves homogalacturonan, specifically methyl-esterified α-D-galacturonan, via β-elimination, producing unsaturated methyl-esterified oligogalacturonides. (3) Genes annotated as “Rhamnogalacturonan lyase” were assigned to PL4\_4, which is listed to have EC 4.2.2.23 rhamnogalacturonate endolyase activity. It performs endo type eliminative cleavage of rhamnogalacturonan I backbone linkages, generating unsaturated rhamnogalacturonate oligosaccharides. Our substrate search of the dbCAN-sub listings found no enzymes known to target pectin oligosaccharide in cynipoids. 

A fourth bacterially derived gene was also discovered during the course of this study and found in all cynipid tribes except Eschatocerini and Synergini. This gene family was originally identified from a member containing only a bacterial B-lectin domain. Broader searches for homologous genes connected it to a gene family with a more complex domain composition. In its most complete form, this gene includes domains annotated as PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and a tandem pair of B-lectin domains (Supplement 5). Bacterial PI-PLC proteins cleave phosphatidylinositol as single-domain units (Heinz et al. 1995, 1998). The GH30\_3 domain corresponds to EC 3.2.1.75, glucan endo-1,6-β-glucosidase, which hydrolyzes β-1,6 linkages in β-1,6-glucans and β-1,6-oligoglucosides. The B-lectin domains are consistent with D-mannose-binding lectin domains of the bulb-type lectin family.

## Outgroup matches were found in diverse bacterial and eukaryotic groups

Outgroup sequences were screened in the NCBI nr, CAZy, and CAZyme3D databases to identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome. Our outgroup screens identified 308 unique PCWDE outgroup sequences (Table S3). Because we discovered an additional putatively bacterial domain in the full B-lectin gene family, outgroup screens were conducted separately for PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3 and B-lectin. A variety of matches were found for each domain individually, but only one outgroup sequence contained the full intact domain architecture including PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and B-lectin: bacterial species *Actinoplanes awajinensis*. A full accounting of the methods and results of the outgroup scan appears in Supplement 3\.  

Our outgroup search for each gene recovered a variety of eukaryotic representatives in addition to a large bacterial contingent. The pattern of gene presence and absence across the tree of life provides strong support for their acquisition through horizontal gene transfer. GH5\_2 and PL4\_4 are completely absent from the immediate phylogenetic neighbors of Cynipoidea, including all hymenopteran and dipteran references (Figure 1). One PCWDE family, PL1\_8, is present in *Contarinia nasturtii* (Swede midge)*, Habropoda laboriosa* (southeastern blueberry bee)*,* and *Ceratina calcarata* (spurred small carpenter bee), but absent in other cecidomyiid and apid genomes (Figure 1; Figure S2). In each family, we found a variety of eukaryotic occurrences which may represent additional horizontal transfer events not previously considered in the literature (Table S3). B-lectin is found in all cynipid tribes except Eschatocerini and Synergini. For contrast, we show the gene count of ancestrally present eukaryotic glycoside hydrolase gene GH1\_e72, which is present nearly universally and varies only in copy number.

## PCWDEs were obtained from various bacteria, retained by gall-associated wasps

With the exception of the *B. kinseyi* reference, which retained the NCBI annotation, genomes were uniformly annotated with Helixer and screened for bacterially transferred gene family members. To confirm gene absence, an additional scan using tblastn was also conducted. Of the 48 cynipoid genomes in our filtered set, PCWDEs are found in the gall-associated clades of Cynipoidea and are generally absent in the figitid clade that transitioned to a parasitoid lifestyle. GH5\_2 is present in 37 genomes, PL1\_8 in 38, PL4\_4 in 36, and B-lectin in 23 (Table S3). The gall-inducing family Diplolepididae possesses all three PCWDEs with the exception of *Pediaspis aceris*, which lacks GH5\_2 (Figure 1). In Figitidae, the gall-inhabiting parasitoid *Euceroptres primus* retains PL1\_8 and GH5\_2, but lacks PL4\_4. All other species, which are free-living entomophagous parasitoids, lack intact copies of all three genes. A pseudogenized copy of PL1\_8 was found in *Parnips nigripes*, alignable to intact queries over 300 residues at \~55% identity but containing multiple frameshifts and internal stops. Cynipids include multiple galling and inquiline lineages, and all of them retain all three PCWDEs, though in two cases evidence is weak. In herb galler *Eubothrus centaureae*, PL1\_8 is detected by tblastn but not annotated by Helixer. In inquiline *Ceroptres kovalevi,* PL4\_4 is absent in both annotation and tblastn, but the Trinity assembly of congener *Ceroptres* ex *Druon ignotum* does contain this gene. None of our target genes were found in Paraulacidae (sister to the rest of Cynipoidea), although the low genome BUSCO completeness (15.3%) of the only representative of that family leaves this result inconclusive. By contrast, the B-lectin gene was found only in Cynipidae. Further, detection of this gene by Helixer was less consistent, and many more occurrences were detected only by tblastn, although every genome in which the gene was present had at least one Helixer hit. Within Cynipidae, the gene was present in Aulacideini, Phanacini, Qwaqwaini, Diastrophini, Ceroptresini, Aylacini, and Cynipini, but absent in *Eschatocerus*, *Aulacidea* and Synergini. This retention and loss pattern does not correspond to any obvious difference in life history.

To place cynipoid sequences relative to bacterial outgroup lineages, we aligned a curated set of catalytic domain amino acid sequences and inferred a phylogeny for each gene family. Full methods and statistics for retained copies in each domain analysis are provided in Supplement 4\. Phylogenetic trees for GH5\_2 and PL1\_8 place the cynipoid lineages as directly sister to bacterial-dominated sister lineages (96% and 82% UFBoot, respectively), with other eukaryotic outgroup genes scattered throughout the tree (Figures 2 and S3). In PL4\_4, the closest siblings of the cynipoid sequences are found in beetles (98% UFBoot), but both are siblings to a single bacterial sequence from *Erwinia tracheiphila*, a plant pathogen (99% UFBoot). In PL1\_8, the cynipoid sequences are sister to a larger lineage dominated by phytopathogenic Pectinobacteriaceae (*Dickeya, Brenneria, Pectobacterium*) and Erwiniaceae (*Erwinia, Pantoea*). In GH5\_2, cynipoid sequences fall in a lineage primarily composed of marine Gammaproteobacteria (*Microbulbifer*) and soil-associated Betaproteobacteria (*Massilia, Eleftheria*). Most bacterial neighbors of cynipoid GH5\_2 proteins include a pair of CBM6 domains. These accessory domains are absent in all cynipoid members of this family, suggesting domain loss occurred during or shortly after transfer.   
Independent trees for PI-PLC and GH30 recover different neighbors (Figure S4). The siblings of PI-PLC are composed almost entirely of *Actinoplanes* spp., while GH30 siblings include *Actinoplanes,* a variety of other Actinomycetes, and *Terriglobus roseus*, all broadly known to be soil bacteria. Aside from heterogeneous exceptions known from marine contexts, all closely related bacterial outgroups for each of the four gene families are found in soil and plant-associated contexts. They are not lineages known to be insect endosymbionts.

## Each gene has one or two modal chromosome placements

To identify the original insertion site and track patterns of duplications and translocations of each gene across Cynipoidea, we conducted chromosome-scale regional homology analysis. The majority of cynipoid target proteins were successfully assigned to *B. kinseyi* chromosomes by RagTag or JCVI contig alignment (Table S5): 78.1% of GH5\_2 proteins were placed (146/187), 85.8% of PL1\_8 (188/219), and 75.9% of PL4\_4 (170/224). Of 46 genome-derived B-lectin-family loci, 39 (84.8%) were placed. These chromosome assignments show that each gene family has one or two clear modal regions in Cynipidae (Figure 1). Across cynipid genomes, PL1\_8 is found predominantly on regions equivalent to *B. kinseyi* chromosome 8, PL4\_4 on chromosome 7, GH5\_2 on chromosomes 6 and 10, and B-lectin on chromosomes 3 and 6\. Placements are more varied in families more distant from Cynipini, but the chromosome 8 placement of PL1\_8 is shared across all families, and the chromosome 7 placement of PL4\_4 is shared in Diplolepididae. These placements are consistent with a single original insertion at those regions in the common ancestor of the three families. GH5\_2 placements in Diplolepididae and *Euceroptres* both include chromosome 1, a placement rarely found in Cynipidae. Given the relatively basal placement of these families in the phylogeny, this suggests chromosome 1 may have been the original insertion site in Cynipoidea, while translocations to chromosomes 6 and 10 had occurred by the crown of Cynipidae. However, the original insertion site of GH5\_2 cannot be resolved with certainty. 

B-lectin is found with its full domain structure intact on chromosomes 6 in nearly all cynipid tribes, indicating this is the original placement. An additional placement on chromosome 3 is limited to Cynipini and contains only one intact B-lectin domain. A number of translocations to nonmodal positions occurred in each family (Figures 1 and S2). *Eschatocerus acaciae,* a cynipoid with inconsistent placement in prior published phylogenies but placed as basal to Cynipidae in Hearn et al. (2024), shares the modal cynipid chromosome placements for GH5\_2 and PL4\_4, though its PL1\_8 is unplaced and it lacks the bacterial B-lectin. This is congruent with its published placement in Cynipidae, and suggests the acquisition of B-lectin occurred after its divergence.   
   
To identify true orthologous loci within each family and to verify the absence of genes in lineages where the target proteins weren’t found, we compared flanking gene sequences adjacent to each occurrence of each gene to identify blocks of conserved synteny. A total of 77 flanking gene patterns could be matched across more than one genome (Table S6). Of these, 26 contained target genes in two or more genomes, accounting for 66.3% (448/676) of genome-derived target loci. Target genes in many synteny loci occur in tandem duplications, up to a maximum of 10 per locus. In many cases, synteny blocks recovered by our criteria as independent are found in relatively close proximity; six PL1\_8 blocks were separated by fewer than 20 genes. Synteny blocks were also detected in species lacking target genes, marked as absent in Table S6, giving strong evidence that absence of the gene in those species indicates a block predates insertion or postdates loss, rather than poor assembly quality. Species taxonomically closer to *B. kinseyi* recovered more flanking genes per locus and synteny blocks were less likely to be undetectable even in poor quality assemblies. In the most distant species, the outgroup Platygastroid *T. remus*, only 24/77 cross-genome-detected synteny blocks were recovered, all of which lacked the target protein, supporting the inference that the transfer happened after the divergence of Cynipoidea and Platygastroidea.

To compare this rate of paralog proliferation to comparable genes, we compared the copy number and rate of chromosome translocation by eCAMI sequence cluster (Supplement 8). This category is an automated subgrouping applied uniformly across dbCAN CAZYmes which mitigates the subdivision bias toward well-studied groups that affects dbCAN subfamilies. We found around 200 eCAMI clusters, and relative to these, median copy number rank varied by lineage. In Cynipini, GH5\_2 is 5.5th (97.7th percentile), PL1\_8 is 6th (96.7th), and PL4\_4 is 6th (97.3rd). In other Cynipidae, GH5\_2 is 8.5th (94.9), PL1\_8 is 12th (89.5), and PL4\_4 is 5th (97.3). Therefore, these enzymes are elevated in copy number in herb and oak galling and inquiline wasps well above the baseline imposed by broader genomic patterns. However, this pattern is less consistent outside of Cynipidae. In Diplolepididae, GH5\_2 has the 21st highest copy number (64.3th percentile), PL1\_8 is 7th (96.0), and PL4\_4 is 12th (87.9); *E. primus* GH5\_2 is 40th (40.0), PL1\_8 is 12th (94.1), and PL4\_4 is absent (Table S7). To assess the rate of chromosome translocation, we used the species tree shown in Figure 1 to infer the most parsimonious ancestral *B. kinseyi* reference chromosomes for each eCAMI cluster, then counted jumps to other chromosomes. We found 11 chromosome-level gains of GH5\_2 (4 in Cynipini, 7 in other lineages), 10 gains of PL1\_8 (3 in Cynipini), and 11 gains of PL4\_4 (5 in Cynipini; Figure S8). This rate of gain is in the 89th-90th percentile relative to all eCAMI sequence clusters (Figure S7), but this effect is explained entirely by their modern copy number. Relative to natively eukaryotic eCAMI CAZymes with comparable copy number (median 3-4 paralogs per genome; n=10), the three bacterially derived PCWDEs rank at the 50th, 50th, and 70th percentile (Table S8). Therefore, a high rate of apparent chromosome translocation is best understood as a side effect of selection to retain duplication events, not an independent variable. 

## PCWDE gene structures share conserved intron placements

Because introns do not occur in bacteria, the presence of an intron is strong evidence that a gene is incorporated into the cynipoid genome. While introns are preferentially placed near the end of signal peptides in secreted genes (Tordai and Patthy 2004), their precise placement and phase provide an independent line of evidence to trace the history of a bacterial gene after its insertion. All three PCWDE families contain introns (Figure 3; Table S12), and both phase and placement relative to CDS are conserved within each PCWDE family, indicating that all candidate proteins from each family were the result of a single transfer. Modal intron positions are shared across paralogs regardless of chromosome placement. Among proteins with coherent single-scaffold CDS models, 87.1% (155/178) of GH5\_2 paralogs have an intron at reference amino acid position 18, including 55 placed on chromosome 6 and 48 on chromosome 10\. 41.0% (87/212) of PL1\_8 paralogs contain an intron at reference amino acid position 9\. 38.6% (83/215) of PL4\_4 paralogs have an intron at reference amino acid position 83, and an additional 15.3% (33/215) have one at position 37\. In each case, the modal intron is found in all cynipoid families where the gene is present. Introns in all three PCWDEs are strongly biased toward the N terminus, consistent with a bias toward the signal peptide: 84.6% of introns were observed within the first quarter of the CDS, compared with 34% for all genes. The median relative position of PCWDE introns was 5.5% of the way through the CDS; the genome-wide median was 43.3%.

Intron placement was less informative for B-lectin genes. Only 13% (3/23) of genome-derived B-lectin genes contain any introns, and only one intron position is shared in two genomes. However, incomplete gene models due to inconsistent annotation by Helixer may limit intron discovery, and the loss of the majority of the gene in its chromosome 3 duplication prevents the use of intron placement to independently validate its homology with the chromosome 6 occurrence.

## Horizontally transferred genes take on nucleotide frequencies of wasp genomes

Bacterial and eukaryotic genomes have different baseline nucleotide frequencies, and over time, transferred sequences are expected to drift toward the nucleotide frequency of the recipient. While coding bases are constrained by selection, the third base in each codon can vary more freely because changes there are often synonymous. Therefore, the proportion of GC at the third codon (GC3) can indicate a shift from bacterial baseline to eukaryotic baseline. Within bacteria, all three PCWDE gene families show widely varied rates of GC3 %, with medians above 50%. Wasp genes have a much lower baseline GC3 frequency, with a median of 36% (Table S9). After acquisition by cynipoids, all three gene families now resemble the wasp genome baseline. By family, wasp paralogs of GH5\_2 are 2.6 percentage points above the median while bacterial relatives are 19.1 pp above; wasp copies of PL1\_8 are 4.3 pp below while bacterial relatives are 19.3 pp above, and wasp paralogs of PL4\_4 are 2.8 pp below while bacterial relatives are 16 pp above. For the B-lectin, wasp paralogs are 11.5 pp above the wasp gene median while bacterial relatives are 57 pp above. Full statistics are presented in Supplement S9. 

## Gene phylogenies show paralog duplication within each family

Per family gene phylogenies (Figures 4 and S5) show that repeated phylogenetically deep duplication events have occurred in all three PCWDE families. The placement of GH5\_2 and PL1\_8 sequences from *Diplolepis rosae*, *Euceroptres primus*, and *Eschatocerus acaciae*—taxa external or early-diverging within Cynipidae—among lineages otherwise composed of cynipid members suggests that some of these duplications occurred early in cynipoid evolution. By contrast, PL4\_4 shows extensive duplication only after the divergence of *Eschatocerus* from the remainder of Cynipidae. Within Cynipidae, branches independently recapitulate all or large parts of the phylogeny of the family. In GH5\_2, three such deep duplications exist on chromosome 6 (Figure S5). Proteins on chromosome 10 have also duplicated, but patterns are more consistent with recent divergences within Synergini and Cynipini. PL4\_4 and PL1\_8 each show five duplications ancestral to Cynipidae and at least one more within Cynipini, all primarily localized on their modal chromosomes 7 and 8 respectively but also including a number of translocations to other regions. Paralog proliferation of all three PCWDEs is ancient and ongoing.

## PCWDE Paralogs show tissue-specific expression

To characterize the role of bacterially-transferred genes in modern Cynipini ecology, we dissected adults from seven species and larvae from two species and extracted RNA from secretory glands. We also sequenced whole larvae, either dissected or within gall samples, of 12 species. Quantification of RNA-seq data shows that PCWDE paralog proliferation is associated with expression in multiple secretory tissues (Table S13). Whole-body larval data show that several paralogs of each gene family are expressed in each Cynipid species sampled, including Cynipini gall inducers and *Ceroptres* and *Synergus* inquilines (Figure 4). Tissue-scale data from gall-inducers *D. cinerosa* and *B. kinseyi* larval tissue-level expression reveal that within each larva, distinct paralogs peak in gut and salivary glands (Figure 6). Expression in adult venom glands was only sampled in Cynipini, and varied considerably between species (Table S13). Across all tissues, PL1\_8 expression peaked in larval salivary glands. The gene also had secondary expression in the gut and in adult venom in *D. cinerosa* but not *B. kinseyi*. PL4\_4 is overwhelmingly specific to the gut, with minor expression in salivary gland and Malpighian tubules. Across both species, GH5\_2 is expressed at 83,000-131,000 TPM in larval gut samples (the most expressed gene in guts of both species), 6,000-52,000 TPM in salivary glands, and 1,700-7,600 TPM in adult venom glands. In each gene family, *B. kinseyi* paralogs show extreme tissue specificity, and *D. cinerosa* genes highest in each tissue are recovered as siblings to the corresponding protein in *B. kinseyi* (Figure 4), suggesting that recruitment of particular paralogs to specific tissue contexts has been stable across the evolution of Cynipini. The modest (\~300-500 TPM) and gut-focused expression of GH1\_e72, an ancestral eukaryotic CAZyme, contrasts with the exceptional expression of the bacterial PCWDEs in gut, salivary gland, and venom gland.

## PCWDEs retain catalytic residues

In order for their inferred catalytic activity to contribute to cynipoid ecology, they must retain an intact catalytic site and overall geometry. To evaluate these characteristics, we aligned coding sequences for each PCWDE family and analyzed rates of nonsynonymous substitutions (Table S10). Across families, 64-75.7% of retained codon sites showed evidence of purifying selection, and median omega (rate of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution) ranged from 0.300 to 0.427, indicating most selection was purifying. FUBAR, which tests per-site selection across all branches, identified 3-8 sites per family (0.5-2.4%) with evidence of pervasive diversifying selection. aBSREL, which tests episodic selection across whole structures on particular branches, identified episodic diversifying selection on 12 of 257 GH5\_2, 26 of 300 PL1\_8, and 24 of 354 PL4\_4 terminal branches (4.7-8.7%; Table S10). Catalytic residues were strongly conserved. The expected residue occurred in 246 of 249 GH5\_2 sequences at the acid/base position (98.8%) and 233 of 237 at the nucleophile position (98.3%), all 284 PL1\_8 sequences at the catalytic-arginine position (100%), and 267 of 276 and 260 of 268 PL4\_4 sequences at the proton-abstractor and proton-donor positions, respectively (96.7% and 97.0%; Table S10). Losses of catalytic residues were not concentrated in particular clades of the gene tree in any family (Table S11). Collectively, these results show that stabilizing selection has acted strongly to retain the structures needed for catalytic activity in all three enzyme families, including catalytic sites and overall protein structure.

# Discussion

We find strong evidence of four HGT events in which three PCWDEs and one multi-domain B-lectin protein absent in related insects were transferred from bacteria to Cynipoidea. These PCWDEs are present in both Diplolepididae and Cynipidae (gall inducers and inquilines) and subsequently lost in the majority of Figitidae (entomophagous parasitoids). In PL1\_8 and GH5\_2, at least one copy exists in all three families that shares flanking genes, chromosome regions, and modal intron placement, strongly supporting a single acquisition event preceding the divergence of these families. In PL4\_4, the same evidence links copies in Diplolepididae and Cynipidae (Figures 1 and 3, Table S6). Following phylogenies from Blaimer et al. (2020) and Hearn et al. (2024), this indicates that a single transfer event for each of the three PCWDEs occurred before the divergence of these three families, dated by Blaimer et al. to between 159.1–160.2 mya (Blaimer et al. 2023; Hearn et al. 2024). Given the incompleteness of the available assembly for Paraulacidae, we cannot exclude the possibility that one or more of these transfers occurred at the crown of Cynipoidea (190.5–195 mya), so we conservatively place all three events in the 159–195 mya range. Absence of all three genes in the majority of figitid species implies subsequent gene loss events in that family. The bacterial B-lectin is present in all cynipid tribes excluding Eschatocerini and Synergini, indicating it was acquired around the crown of Cynipidae, a lineage Blaimer et al. (2020) date to 71.6–141.7 mya. These events indicate that ancient Cynipoids were repeatedly exposed to bacterial genes, some of which were subsequently retained across millions of years of evolution. 

## Role of PCWDEs in digestion of plant tissue

That cynipoids acquired three PCWDE genes within a relatively short span of evolutionary time suggests that their ancestral ecological context provided both exposure to bacteria carrying PCWDEs and a selective advantage for retaining them. According to ancestral state reconstructions by Blaimer et al. (2020), when cynipoids acquired these bacterial PCWDEs, they were not gall inducers. Rather, they were either endophytic parasitoids (or potentially hyperparasitoids) of an herbivorous insect, or an inquiline associated with galls induced by another insect. In either case, it is likely that the ancestor of modern cynipoids oviposited into plant tissue. In that context, a transition to herbivory would have offered immediate access to an abundant food source. However, high fiber content would have posed an obstacle to larvae exploiting this resource. Horizontally transferred genes are a proven solution to this problem (Acuña 2012; Pauchet & Heckel 2013; Kirsch 2022), and cynipoids deploy this solution in triplicate to solubilize cellulose, homogalacturonan, and rhamnogalacturonan I simultaneously. 

Because cell wall polymers are made of sugars, enzymes that cleave them could theoretically make these sugars available as an energy source. However, each PCWDE cleaves internal bonds in polymers and generates oligomers of at least two sugars that require further degradation by specialized enzymes to become absorbable sugars (Davies et al. 1998; Jensen et al. 2010; Kirsch et al. 2016). GH5\_2 endo-cleavage of cellulose produces cellodextrins (Davies et al. 1998), and our CAZyme search identified one native cynipid protein that has the potential to further digest cellodextrins. However, the substrate specificity of the cynipoid enzyme is undetermined, and GH1\_e72 was expressed in Cynipini gut tissue only at around 300-500 TPM. This expression level is low relative to the over 100,000 TPM expression of GH5\_2. The low expression and uncertain substrate of GH1\_e72, as well as the apparent absence of pectin oligomer degrading enzymes, do not provide compelling evidence that gut-expressed PCWDEs are a major pathway for converting cell wall polysaccharides into absorbable sugars. In this respect, Cynipoidea resemble the ancestral hymenopteran herbivores in clade Symphyta, which also lack apparent enzymatic pathways that would allow them to obtain energy from pectin, and possess GH1 enzymes whose ability to convert cellodextrin to glucose is uncertain (Fogal and Webb 1976; Ojeda-Martinez et al. 2024; Figure 1).   
   
Even when their breakdown products cannot be metabolized for energy, cleaving cell wall polymers can confer substantial digestive benefits. In the herbivorous beetle *Phaedon cochleariae*, knockout of fungal-derived GH28 polygalacturonases impaired larval growth and survival, which were rescued by supplementation of enzyme but not oligomer. This suggests that pectin degradation is primarily advantageous for its indirect effects on absorption of other nutrients, not for the energetic contents of the pectin polymer (Kirsch et al. 2022). Work in mammals suggests that this may be due to increased viscosity of gut contents caused by soluble pectins, which has been shown to delay starch digestion and glucose absorption (Bai and Gilbert 2022). Cellulose presents a different challenge. As a large component of cell wall biomass, it can significantly dilute nutritionally usable dietary intake. In caterpillars, dietary dilution with cellulose reduces growth, an effect they are able to partially mitigate through compensatory feeding (Lee 2017). In Apocrita, compensatory feeding is limited by the inability to defecate, because intake of higher volumes of tissue directly entails retention of more fiber. For gall wasps, this problem is substantially mitigated by the induction of specialized nutritive tissue, which composes the entirety of their larval diet. Despite their high palatability, nutritive tissue cells retain primary cell wall synthesis during cell division (Bronner 1977; Markel et al. 2024). Later larval stages use mandibles to chew open nutritive tissue, and cell wall polymers are likely prevalent in the imbibed liquid (Bronner 1992; Schönrogge et al. 2000). Therefore, while gall wasps benefit from a nutritionally enriched diet, fiber accumulation still limits consumption. Enzymatic cleavage does not remove sugars from apocritan guts, but transforming them from polymers to oligomers may be sufficient to mitigate this limitation.

PCWDE acquisition from microbes preceded radiations of several major herbivorous insect clades, including GH28 in Euphasmatodea and Phytophaga, GH5\_2 in Cerambycidae, and GH5\_40 in Cicadellidae (Kirsch et al. 2014; Shelomi et al. 2016; McKenna et al. 2019; Shin et al. 2022; Molligan et al. 2026). The role of horizontally transferred PCWDEs in transitions to herbivory among Hymenoptera is less clear. Superfamilies Chalcidoidea and Ichneumoidea contain independent transitions to herbivory (Munro et al. 2011; Samacá-Sáenz et al. 2022; Cruaud et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025b), but although cellulase activity has been documented in one chalcidoid (Ishaaya & Plaut 1974), the presence, origin, and expression of PCWDEs in these lineages remain poorly characterized. In some lineages, other strategies are employed to limit larval consumption of cell wall polymers. In *Apis*, adults digest pollen and provision young larvae with glandular secretions (Wright et al. 2018). The consistent pattern among apocritan herbivores is that herbivorous larvae feed on specialized, nutrient-rich tissues (Tooker & Giron 2020). Within Cynipoidea, however, exposure to plant cell walls is tightly correlated with retention of their three bacterially acquired PCWDEs. By solubilizing cellulose, homogalacturonan, and rhamnogalacturonan I and reducing their capacity to obstruct diffusion, these enzymes may have relaxed the digestive constraints imposed by blind midgut anatomy, facilitating the transition to herbivory that preceded the evolution of gall induction in Diplolepididae and Cynipidae (Blaimer et al. 2020).

## PCWDEs were lost in lineages not associated with galls

While the majority of modern cynipoids are either inquilines or inducers of galls, the majority of Figitid species (\~1,700) are entomophagous parasitoids with no association with galls or plants in general. The retention of PL1\_8 and GH5\_2 in an early branching Figitid, *Euceroptres primus*, supported by the pseudogene of PL1\_8 in *Parnips nigripes*, indicates that these genes were present in the ancestral members of this family and lost at least twice. PL4\_4 was lost at least once, possibly at the root of the family. The retention of PCWDEs in some members of Figitidae is closely linked with their life history. Dissection and micro-CT work (Brown et al. 2026\) has demonstrated that *Euceroptres* *primus* is a parasitoid and feeds directly on gall-inducer larvae. Despite this shift to entomophagy, *E. primus* retains two of the three bacterial PCWDEs. This suggests that cell wall polymers may remain digestively relevant for gall-inhabiting entomophages. Such parasitoids may encounter cell wall polymers either in prey gut contents or in direct ingestion of nutritive cells during feeding. By contrast, the majority of parasitoids in Figitidae do not feed within plant tissue, and many of their host insects are not herbivores. Free-living entomophagous parasitoids therefore likely experience relaxed selection to retain PCWDEs, allowing their subsequent loss. 

## Venom and salivary paralogs may modulate plant response to damage

The consistency with which gall-associated lineages retained all three genes underlines the importance of these genes in that context. Subsequent duplications indicate that they are versatile tools that have found additional applications. Cynipini, which have the largest genomes in Cynipoidea and likely in Hymenoptera more generally, show especially extensive gene duplications, both as tandem arrays and as translocations to other chromosomes (Figure 1). Prior experimental work in Cerambycidae has shown that GH5\_2 enzymes can diversify to target xylan, xyloglucan, cellulose, and mannan (Shin et al. 2022). In Cynipini, enzymes retain residues consistent with catalytic activity, but substrate specificity remains undetermined. Instead, the clear distinction between paralogs is in their tissue-specific expression. Gut expression of GH5\_2 is substantial, representing 8-13% of protein-coding expression in that tissue, consistent with a role solubilizing abundant polymers. However, other paralogs are highly expressed in larval salivary gland (GH5\_2, PL4\_4, PL1\_8) and adult venom gland (GH5\_2, PL1\_8) of some species. Larval salivary gland expression of all three PCWDE families has also been reported in *Diplolepis* *rosae* (Mozhaitseva et al. 2023), indicating that deployment of these genes outside the gut is not restricted to Cynipini. Both venom and salivary expression may be explicable through predigestive functions, loosening cell adhesion and softening cell walls before larval feeding. However, venom is applied many months before larval feeding in some species, so digestion is unlikely to be the entire role of venom expression. Additionally, cell-wall fragments are a known developmental signal in plants, so digestion may not be the only function of these tissue-specific paralogs. 

While PCWDEs are used antagonistically by herbivores to degrade the structure of plant tissue, similar enzymes are also deployed by plants to remodel and repair, which may give us clues to their expression in organs outside of the gut. Physically wounded plants express GH9B β-1,4-glucanases to facilitate cell-wall reconstruction (Notaguchi et al. 2020). This gene expression is part of a wound-repair program (Ikeuchi et al. 2022), and also includes pectate lyase-like enzymes, which directly enable cell expansion (Ye et al. 2025). Exogenous addition of glucanase to wound sites facilitates grafting success (Yang et al. 2024), in part by improving cell-cell adhesion (Kawakatsu et al. 2020). Further, exogenous addition of cellulase and pectinase enzymes in combination with physical wounding has been shown to directly trigger changes in plant gene expression associated with callus formation and vascular regeneration (Zhang et al. 2022). Exogenous addition of pectin-derived oligogalacturonides has been shown to induce wound healing, shoot formation, and flower formation (Lu et al. 2021; Falasca et al. 2008; Marfà et al. 1991). Therefore, prior work shows that PCWDEs similar to those expressed in cynipoid venom and saliva may elicit plant remodeling both through physical loosening of cell walls and through perception of polysaccharide fragments generated by these enzymes.

Substrate specificities and signalling pathways in cynipoids may differ, but this research offers a set of clear testable hypotheses about the roles these enzymes play in Cynipini non-gut expression. Venom-applied GH5\_2 β-1,4-glucanases and PL1\_8 pectin lyases could facilitate tissue lysis known to form cavities around cynipoid eggs (Bronner 1973; Shorthouse et al. 2005), promote cell adhesion during wound repair to seal eggs inside, or initiate changes in gene expression that make the tissue more competent for gall induction. Salivary expression of the same pair of PCWDEs may contribute to the gall’s continual renewal of the feeding interface despite ongoing larval feeding damage. More specifically, they may aid in the maturation of nutritive tissue cells, which show thinning cell walls (Hough, 1953\) and expanded cell volume as they differentiate from parenchyma (Harper et al. 2004). In combination, these possibilities suggest that the acquisition of PCWDEs from bacteria offered cynipoids not only the capacity to digest plant material, but also a new set of handles to modify the structure and developmental trajectory of living plant cells. 

## Acquisition of a multi-domain B-lectin protein and subsequent domain loss

While the intact form of the fourth bacterially transferred gene does contain a glycoside hydrolase domain, it does not fit clearly in the PCWDE category. Its inferred ancestral form contained four distinct domains: a membrane-lipid-cleaving phospholipase, an endo-β-1,6-glucanase, and two bulb-type B-lectins. Domain architecture evidence suggests it was acquired near the crown of family Cynipidae likely from a soil or plant-associated bacterium, potentially an actinomycete. The known targets of GH30\_3 enzymes are β-1,6-glucans, which are typical components of fungal cell walls (Temple et al. 2017). In the outer wall of many fungi, β-1,6-glucan polysaccharides connect mannoproteins to the underlying β-1,3-glucan and chitin scaffold, and their cleavage can affect the structural integrity of the cell wall (Kollár et al. 1997; Wang et al. 2024). Characterized members of the bulb-type B-lectins have been shown to bind mannose-rich glycans like those found in yeast mannan (Kaku et al. 1992), so tandem B-lectins may complement this enzyme’s function by localizing it to mannoproteins. The relevance of the PI-PLC domain remains unclear. Therefore, while the molecular and ecological targets of this enzyme can only be discussed speculatively, the combination of domains present suggests an enzyme that localizes to mannose-rich fungal surfaces and cleaves structural β-1,6-glucan. 

Such a function would be consistent with the known ecology of a possible actinomycete donor. Actinomycete strains selected for antagonism toward glucan-walled microorganisms commonly produce extracellular glucanases capable of cell-wall hydrolysis and lysis (Valois et al. 1996; Fayad et al. 2001). Fungal interactions are also a relevant ecological concern for gall-associated wasps. Endophytic fungi are known to colonize cynipid galls (Wilson 1995\) and chamber-invading fungi can cause substantial larval mortality in gall inducers (Cooper and Rieske 2010). Fungal cell wall degradation is therefore ecologically plausible in both the bacterial donor and the recipient wasp. Tests of functional activity through binding assays and fungal growth inhibition assays, as well as differential expression in the presence and absence of fungal infection of galls, would help clarify the role of this gene. 

The subsequent history of retention and expression of this gene provides little support for a conserved antifungal function. The multidomain form was retained intact in Ceroptresini, Diastrophini, and Cynipini, but appears to have been lost entirely in Synergini. *Ceroptres* larvae express the domain-complete form, but with limited sampling, this is difficult to interpret. Fungal ecology has been studied in too few cynipid systems to make predictions about its retention as a putative antifungal gene across tribes. A fungal cell wall cleavage explanation is further complicated by the observation that in Cynipini, duplication and domain remodeling produced a second paralog retaining only the B-lectin carbohydrate-binding domain. All detectable expression in our Cynipini tissue datasets was associated with this noncatalytic paralog. Any explanation of the functional role of this gene in Cynipini ecology must account for its continued utility in the absence of catalytic activity. Nonetheless, repeated losses, domain reduction, and modest, tissue-variable expression provide an informative contrast to the broad retention, repeated expansion, and high expression of all three PCWDE families. 

## Ecological context and unresolved mechanism of bacterial transfer

Confident identification of specific bacterial donors in ancient HGT events is challenged both by subsequent HGT among bacteria and by extinction of donor lineages (Szöllősi et al. 2013). We find several consistent candidate donor groups at higher taxonomic scales, but given the 159-195 million years elapsed since inferred transfer, identification of a single donor lineage is unreliable. For all four transferred gene families, the bacterial sequences closest to cynipoid sequences belong to lineages known to be associated with soil and plant tissue, including Erwiniaceae, Pectobacteriaceae, Oxalobacteraceae, Cellvibrionales, and Actinoplanes. Given the taxonomic diversity of candidate donors, it is unlikely that all four horizontally transferred genes, or even all three PCWDEs, originated from a single donor lineage. Instead, the common feature across donor lineages is the shared ecological context of soil and plant tissue. 

While the importance of bacterial HGT as a mechanism for enabling life history transitions in insects is now well established, the mechanism by which such genes enter insect genomes is unknown. Many insects host germline intracellular infections like *Wolbachia*, which offer direct opportunities for heritable genetic exchange. However, none of our candidate donor lineages are known to be germline endosymbionts. This is consistent with the pattern observed in other bacterially derived PCWDEs in insects. In the best-resolved cases, inferred bacterial donors are plant-associated or polysaccharide-degrading bacteria rather than obligate insect symbionts: *Streptomyces*\-like actinobacteria for a coffee-berry-borer GH10 xylanase (Padilla-Hurtado et al. 2012), *Cellvibrio*/*Teredinibacter*\-like Gammaproteobacteria for *Phaedon* GH11 xylanases (Pauchet & Heckel 2013), and gut- or food-associated gammaproteobacteria for phasmatodean GH28 polygalacturonases (Shelomi et al. 2016\)*.* On the grounds that cynipoids were ancestrally endophytic parasitoids or inquilines, we hypothesize that the most likely ecological context for gene transfer was exposure of an egg to bacteria during oviposition into nonsterile plant tissues containing insect hosts. How bacterial DNA entered and became heritably inserted in germline chromosomes remains unresolved.

## Conclusion

Our results reconstruct an ancient and recurring history of gene transfer from environmental bacteria to cynipoid wasps, confirming prior reports that this lineage possesses a diverse repertoire of bacterially acquired PCWDE families. Cynipoids acquired and retained three complementary enzyme families with predicted activities against cellulose and the two major pectic backbones, homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan-I. Cynipoid larval midguts cannot expel undigested cell wall polysaccharides as frass during feeding, and horizontally transferred PCWDEs may have mitigated the constraint this posed on dietary intake. These acquisitions preceded the diversification of herbivorous cynipoids into one of the few species-rich herbivorous lineages in Apocrita, including major radiations of gall-inducing wasps. Our comparative dataset documents the post-transfer history of these genes, showing a pattern of tandem duplication and chromosomal translocation in gall-associated lineages and losses in entomophagous lineages. In modern Cynipini, horizontally transferred PCWDEs represent a major component of digestive gene expression. Distinct paralogs are also expressed in salivary and venom glands, where they interact directly with living plant tissue and may have acquired novel functions related to gall induction. A fourth bacterial gene, originally encoding a complex protein with multiple catalytic domains, was acquired later and expressed in Cynipini as a noncatalytic B-lectin paralog. This contrasting outcome demonstrates how horizontally acquired genes can undergo substantial functional transformation. The exceptional preservation and prominent digestive expression of PCWDEs suggest their sustained importance to cynipoid herbivory.

# Materials and Methods

## Genome structure and annotation

The candidate panel was composed of 69 cynipoid genomes and one outgroup, *Telenomus remus* (Platygastroidea: Scelionidae), including 50 assemblies downloaded from NCBI, 19 assembled from DNA reads on ENA, and one newly sequenced assembly (Table S1). Of these, we chose *Belonocnema kinseyi* (NCBI accession GCF\_010883055.1) as our reference genome. In order to improve chromosome-scale coverage in the early diverging part of family Figitidae, we replaced an existing genome of *Euceroptres primus* with a new chromosomal-scale assembly following the protocol outlined in Zhang et al. 2026, including HiFi and Hi-C (Figure S1). Full methods and detailed description are presented in Supplement 1\. In order to verify the absence of each gene family in the ancestors of Cynipoidea, we also downloaded 15 protein sets for a representative panel of Hymenoptera and Diptera. These were used for whole-genome protein detection only. To prevent bias in our analyses from incomplete genomes, we set a 40% minimum BUSCO completeness threshold to retain the most complete available genomes representing each cynipoid lineage. 

To assign comparable spatial coordinates across assemblies of varying contiguity, we used RagTag to align all non-chromosome scale genomes to the 10 chromosome-scale scaffolds of the GCF\_010883055.1 *B. kinseyi* assembly, numbered by length in decreasing order, as reference (Alonge 2022). Genes on scaffolds that could not be assigned by synteny were labeled “Unplaced.” For *Euceroptres primus,* which was assembled to 6 chromosome-scale scaffolds, JCVI v1.5.11 was used to assign target genes to homologous *B. kinseyi* chromosomes (Tang et al. 2024). JCVI was also used to match the ten *Dryocosmus kuriphilus* chromosomes to their homologs in *B. kinseyi*. References to chromosomes 1-10 refer to genomic blocks based on the 10 *B. kinseyi* pseudochromosomes and are not intended as claims about karyotype. 

Since only three of the genomes were previously annotated, Helixer v0.3.6 (model invertebrate\_v0.3\_m\_0100; Stiehler et al. 2021\) was used to predict genomic elements. NCBI annotations were retained only for *B. kinseyi*. 

## Ortholog catalog and synteny

An orthology catalog was initially constructed from 667,137 proteins from 41 genome proteomes after excluding proteins for which at least 30% of CDS bases were repeat-masked. Proteins were assigned to orthogroups, designated CynOGs, with OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms 2019). Profile HMMs were constructed for CynOGs containing at least three members. Proteins from nine later-added or replacement proteomes and proteins predicted from transcriptomes were searched against this library at E \< 1 × 10⁻⁵ and assigned to the CynOG with the best full-sequence E-value and score. CynOGs were used to investigate the flanking gene context of horizontally transferred gene loci. 

We created an index listing CynOG assignments in their order of appearance along each RagTag pseudochromosome. Synteny blocks were identified by counting flanking genes at windows of 5, 15, and 50 on either side of each target gene, excluding duplicates of the target itself. Pairwise synteny between two loci of a given family was assessed using the Jaccard index of their flanking CynOG signatures. Locus pairs with Jaccard ≥0.15 at any window size were considered syntenic. Synteny blocks were identified by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on the narrow-window (±5 genes) Jaccard distance matrix (1 \- J), cutting the dendrogram at distance 0.85 (J \= 0.15). After shared synteny blocks were defined, we looked for each conserved flanking gene pattern across all 49 genomes. After a block was identified, we rescanned all gene models within the search region for target hits. When a match was discovered, the genome contained a target hit at that locus, and the number of tandem repeats was counted; if it didn’t, the locus was marked EMPTY. If fewer than 3 conserved flanking genes could be found in a cluster, the block was marked NOT\_DETERMINED. Full orthology and synteny methods are provided in Supplement 6\. 

## Transcript data

Transcriptome data was used to supplement taxonomic coverage and to characterize the tissue context of gene expression. We produced RNA-seq data for larval gut, salivary glands, and Malpighian tubules of two species and adult venom and accessory glands of seven species. We additionally sequenced whole body larvae and adult carcasses. In Cynipini, accessory glands produce fluid that coats eggs during oviposition, while venom glands produce fluid injected into host plants surrounding the egg (Guiguet et al. 2023). Trinity assemblies were created for species lacking genomes. Before quantification, reads were sorted by species using BBSplit (BBMap v39.37; Bushnell 2014\) to remove oak reads and identify parasitoid and inquiline wasp contamination, which is common among wild gall wasps (Table S2). Samples containing insect reads predominantly contributed by non-inducer species were split. Chalcidoid parasitoid samples were discarded, while cynipid inquiline samples were retained, assembled, and included to increase phylogenetic coverage. In order to test whether tissue-level expression is correlated with chromosome-specific paralog duplication, *Disholcaspis cinerosa* target genes were matched to *B. kinseyi* paralogs using gene tree topology. Cynipoid reads were quantified against genome-derived transcript sets or Trinity assemblies with Salmon v1.10.3 (Patro et al. 2017). Expression values are given as transcripts per million (TPM), normalized to the total transcripts per sample. Complete collection, extraction, and bioinformatics details are provided in Supplement 2\.

## Target gene identification

While the three PCWDE genes had previously been identified by gene name, more precise enzyme family assignments were needed to identify target chemistry and potential bacterial donors. All predicted proteins were scanned against the dbCAN HMM database (Yin et al 2012\) at the subfamily level (dbCAN-sub v5.2.4; Zheng et al 2023\) using HMMER3.4 hmmsearch to assign CAZy classifications and domain boundaries (Cantarel et al. 2009), at a threshold of E \< 10^-10. Subfamily assignments were validated by submitting a representative LocalColabFold-predicted structure to the CAZyme3D database (Siva Shanmugam and Yin, 2025). After enzyme type information allowed us to predict specific oligosaccharides generated by each PCWDE, we searched the dbCAN-sub listed substrates of other CAZy hits to identify enzymes potentially capable of degrading the oligosaccharides they generate as products. 

During preliminary search, we identified a fourth *B. kinseyi* gene of apparently bacterial origin: a compact B-lectin carbohydrate-binding module. dbCAN HMM search was inadequate to identify all members of this gene family because domain intactness varied and many eukaryotic B-lectin genes exist. To identify only genes of the apparently bacterial B-lectin, we used the membership in the CynOG containing the *B. kinseyi* gene, including assigned transcripts, then validated that they contained at least the B-lectin domain. For each protein, full domain boundaries were assigned using HMMER3 hmmscan against the Pfam-A database (Mistry et al. 2021). 

Some genomes may contain evidence of bacterial genes too degraded to recover in annotation. To detect these pseudogene fragments, a representative sequence for each gene family was used as a tblastn v2.16.0+ (Camacho et al. 2009\) query against all 70 retained and 5 alternate excluded assemblies. To avoid false positives, tblastn hits above a 45% identity floor and family-specific identity thresholds (Supplement 3\) were treated as provisional and results were evaluated only for genome-gene family combinations lacking an accepted Helixer/dbCAN family member. HSP findings were queried against CynOGs and hits that didn’t match the corresponding PCWDE gene were removed. Hits below the 45% identity floor were considered as possible pseudogenes only where other hits for that family and genome were not found. Pseudogene status was assigned based on the presence of reading frame disruptions and premature stop codons. 

To identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome, outgroup sequences were identified by querying the NCBI database with a representative *B. kinseyi* protein sequence, identifying all listed subfamily members on [CAZy.org](http://CAZy.org), and querying CAZyme3D with a representative LocalColabFold-predicted structure. Full gene identification and outgroup sequence gathering methods are provided in Supplement 3\. 

## Gene Phylogenies

To infer gene phylogenies, we trimmed sequences to the dbCAN hmmsearch-predicted target domain plus 50 flanking amino acids. Proteins with unusual length or domain architecture were excluded. Two sets of protein trees were created for each gene family: transfer-context trees inferred from the complete cynipoid-plus-outgroup sets (Figures 2 and S3), and cynipoid locus trees rooted on a bacterial outgroup to resolve post-transfer duplication history (Figures 4 and S5). To generate these phylogenies, sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Maximum-likelihood protein trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (Wong et al. 2026\) under a fixed LG+G4 model. Branch support was estimated with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates (-B 1000; Hoang et al. 2018). For B-lectin domain proteins, because many contained more than one copy of the domain, tree building was conducted in two stages. First, all B-lectin domains were isolated and a tree was constructed as above (Figure S6). In that tree, each domain was assigned to either a branch containing the N-terminal or C-terminal outgroup B-lectin. Subsequently, these two branches were aligned as partitions and analyzed as a single tree. Full phylogeny methods are provided in Supplements 4 and 5\.

## Gene domestication and intron analysis

Bacterial genes are transformed in several ways after integration into eukaryotic genomes. The insertion of an intron is strong evidence of integration into wasp genomes and offers an independent line of evidence to trace each gene after transfer. Intron positions were extracted from gene model predictions for each target hit in each genomic reference in which they were found. For each family, a single *B. kinseyi* reference protein was selected. All other genomic homologs were aligned to that reference with Biopython's PairwiseAligner (Cock et al. 2009\) and each intron was assigned an amino acid position relative to the coordinates of that sequence. Intron placements were clustered at a maximum distance of three amino acids. For each intron placement cluster, the dominant splice phase, splice signal, and chromosomal distribution of genes with that intron site were recorded. 

Nucleotide composition also differs in eukaryotic and bacterial genomes. Bacteria have variable and often high GC content, while eukaryotic genes tend to have more uniform nucleotide composition and lower GC content. Because third position codon changes are largely synonymous, they are less constrained by selection and may change more freely after transfer. Therefore, to answer this question we compared the GC content of the third base per codon, GC3. We tested GC3 content of wasp genes and compared that to the wasp and bacterial statistics per gene family. We also conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to evaluate whether, for each species, the HGT gene is significantly closer to the host gene median GC3% than the closest bacterial relatives from our gene tree. Complete details about intron and GC3 analysis are given in Supplement 9\. 

## Catalytic residue identification and selection analysis

After insertion into eukaryotic genomes, bacterial genes are exposed to new selection pressures and may experience losses of catalytic capacity, altered binding site geometry, or changed surface chemistry. To confirm that each protein retained the experimentally determined catalytic residues for its family, sequence alignment was used to identify catalytic amino acid positions. Catalytic residues were defined from experimentally characterized family members (Supplement 10). Reference sequences and the locked cynipoid domain sequences were aligned de novo with MAFFT v7.526. Each mapped position was classified as containing the expected reference residue, a substitution, or a gap/ambiguous residue (Table S10). 

To generate codon alignments for selection analyses, we retrieved the coding sequence corresponding to each retained cynipoid protein. Coding sequences were threaded onto protein alignments and maximum-likelihood trees were inferred from the filtered codon alignments with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under GTR+G4 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Hoang et al. 2018).

To identify branches with evidence of episodic diversifying selection within Cynipoidea, we used aBSREL (Smith et al. 2015\) in HyPhy v2.5.85 (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020). Each branch was tested independently for a proportion of sites with omega greater than 1, and significance was assessed at the HyPhy Holm-corrected p \< 0.05 threshold. Because bacterial outgroups are too divergent for reliable codon-level comparison, per-site selection analysis was restricted to cynipoid sequences. FUBAR (Murrell et al. 2013\) was run in HyPhy v2.5.85. Sites were classified as under purifying or diversifying selection at posterior probability \> 0.9; all other retained sites were classified as neutral. Full catalytic residue and selection analysis methods are provided in Supplement 10\.
