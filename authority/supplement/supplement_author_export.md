
## S1. A chromosome-scale genome for *Euceroptres primus*

A new chromosome-scale genome assembly was generated for the figitid species *Euceroptres primus.* Preliminary work showed that this was the only sampled figitid to retain the bacterial PCWDEs. Confident resolution of the chromosomal context of each gene in this species provides important evidence about the timing and placement of the original lateral transfer events. The assembly was generated following the protocol outlined in Zhang et al (2026). Samples were reared from *Andricus quercuspetiolicola* galls on *Quercus bicolor* in Pennsylvania, collected in 2025\. A single female wasp was homogenized and used for high-molecular-weight DNA extraction, sheared to \~18 kb, and prepared into a PacBio SMRTbell library with barcoded adapters, then sequenced on a PacBio Revio system; concurrently, a pool of three females was used to prepare a Hi-C library using the enzymes DdeI and DpnII and was subsequently sequenced on an Element AVITI system. HiFi reads were filtered using FCS-Adaptor and HiFiAdapterFilt (Sim et al. 2022), assembled with HiFiASM (Cheng et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022\) using the insect telomere motif (Vitkova et al. 2005), purged of duplicates with PurgeDups (Guan et al. 2020), and scaffolded with Hi-C reads via BWAmem2 (Vasimuddin et al. 2019), Picard (Broad Institute 2019), and YaHS (Zhou et al. 2023), with manual editing in Juicebox (Durand et al. 2016). Contig coverage was assessed with Minimap2 (Li 2018), completeness with BUSCO (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025\) using hymenoptera\_odb12. Taxonomic assignments were assessed with BLAST+ (Camacho et al. 2009), Diamond (Buchfink et al. 2021), and FCS-GX (Astashyn et al. 2024), summarized with Blobtools2 (Challis et al. 2020\) and blobblurb, to remove non-arthropod and *Wolbachia* contigs. A transcriptome from a single female was sequenced, with reads filtered by Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014\) and expression quantified with Salmon (Patro et al. 2017). Genome annotation was performed using EGAPx with STAR, Miniprot, and Gnomon, supplemented by BRAKER3 (Gabriel et al. 2024\) with RepeatModeler and OrthoDB v12, while genomic elements were derived with bedtools (Quinlan and Hall 2010\) and Pfam domains assigned via HMMER (Eddy 2011; Mistry et al. 2021).

PacBio HiFi sequencing yielded a highly contiguous genome for *Euceroptres primus*. From one Revio cell where the barcoded *E. primus* SMRTBell library represented one third of the sequencing run, 2,706,303 HiFi reads (37.95 Gb of HiFi data) were obtained, of which 30 (0.0011% of reads) were discarded after filtering with HiFiAdapterFilt. The genome coverage was estimated at 44.6× coverage as reported by GenomeScope2.

The final genome consisted of 15 scaffolds totaling 839 Mb, an N50 of 155 Mb, L50 of 3, N90 of 107 Mb, and L90 of 6 (Figure S1). The assembly has a fairly complete hymenoptera\_odb12 BUSCO, C:97.4% \[S: 96.5%, D:0.9%\], F:0.8%, M:1.8% (n \= 5,920). Hi-C scaffolding revealed six chromosomes (Figure S1) with only 8 contigs representing 970 kb (0.12%) of the genome remaining unplaced, and the genome was mostly homozygous. EGAPx annotated 14,633 genes, including 12,973 protein-coding genes and 1,737 lncRNA.

## S2. Genome panel and transcriptome data

A total of 70 genomes were considered for inclusion (Table S1). The candidate panel was composed of 69 cynipoid genomes and one outgroup, *Telenomus remus* (Platygastroidea: Scelionidae). In addition to the new *Euceroptres primus* genome (S1), a set of 40 cynipoid assemblies and *T. remus* were downloaded from NCBI in October 2025\. Additionally, whole-genome DNA reads were downloaded from ENA (18) and NCBI (1) and assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 (Bankevich et al. 2012). Finally, an additional 9 assemblies were downloaded from NCBI in July 2026\. Of these, we chose *Belonocnema kinseyi* (NCBI accession GCF\_010883055.1) as our reference genome. This accession was originally uploaded as *Belonocnema treatae*, before the Texas population from which the samples were collected was re-described as *B. kinseyi* (Zhang et al. 2021). In order to verify the absence of each gene family in the ancestors of Cynipoidea, we also downloaded 15 protein sets for a representative panel of Hymenoptera and Diptera. These were used for whole genome protein detection only and excluded from chromosome-scale analysis. BUSCO v6.0 was used to evaluate the completeness of genomes relative to the hymenoptera\_odb12 protein set (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025). Since only three of the genomes (*B. kinseyi*, *Leptopilina boulardi* and *L. heterotoma*) were previously annotated, Helixer v0.3.6 (model invertebrate\_v0.3\_m\_0100; Stiehler et al. 2021\) was used to predict genomic elements. NCBI annotations were retained only for *B. kinseyi*. Of the genomes we considered, 12 were removed because they fell below a 40% BUSCO completeness threshold. Taxonomic redundancy (8) and other assembly quality issues (1) prompted the removal of another 9 genomes, resulting in a core analytic set of 49 annotated genomes (Table S1). 

Genomic data were supplemented with RNA-seq reads of a variety of tissue types and life stages (Table S2a), which were assembled de novo as needed and quantified against genomic or de novo protein predictions. For whole body larvae, whole asexual galls were collected of *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides, Disholcaspis cinerosa, Neuroterus howertoni*, *Druon quercuslanigerum, Andricus quercusfoliatus* (fall 2024, Houston, TX), *B. kinseyi* (fall 2025, Houston, TX), and *Druon ignotum* (September 2025, Brainerd, MN). Larvae were dissected out of fresh galls. For venom expression, asexual galls of *Disholcaspis cinerosa, Bassettia pallida, Callirhytis quercusbatatoides,* and *Neuroterus howertoni* and sexual generation galls of *B. kinseyi* were collected for adult rearing. Shortly after emergence, venom glands and accessory glands were dissected out in 1x PBS using microforceps. In Cynipini, accessory glands produce fluid that coats eggs during oviposition, while venom glands produce fluid injected into host plants surrounding the egg (Guiguet et al. 2023). Venom gland extractions were unsuccessful for *N. howertoni* venom glands and accessory glands were not removed from *C. quercusbatatoides*. Venom glands, accessory glands, and carcass for RNA-seq were collected in fall 2024-winter 2025\. Tissue-specific larval RNA-seq (gut, salivary glands, and Malpighian tubules) were collected in fall 2025 from asexual galls of *Disholcaspis cinerosa* and in winter 2026 from sexual galls of *B. kinseyi*. RNA extraction was conducted using the PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Library prep and RNA-seq were conducted by Novogene. Whole larva and adult venom gland libraries from *Dryocosmus quercuspalustris* were collected during work associated with Martinson et al. 2022 and are presented here for the first time. Finally, publicly available RNA-seq reads were obtained from SRA *Biorhiza pallida* (whole larvae in galls, Hearn et al. 2019), *Neuroterus numismalis,* and *Neuroterus quercusbaccarum* (whole larvae in galls, Bellows et al. 2024).

RNA reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014\) and sorted against a panel of hymenopteran and oak genomes with BBSplit (BBMap v39.37; Bushnell 2014), using minratio=0.56, minhits=1, and ambiguous2=best, to identify parasitoid and inquiline wasp contamination, which is common among wild gall wasps. Samples containing insect reads predominantly contributed by non-inducer species were split by species. Chalcidoid parasitoid samples were discarded, while cynipid inquiline samples were retained, assembled, and included to increase phylogenetic coverage. Reads were assembled per species using Trinity (Grabherr et al. 2011\) for species for which genomes were unavailable. Trinity assemblies were postprocessed with EviGene (Gilbert 2013\) to reduce redundancy and TransDecoder (Haas et al. 2013\) to predict amino acid sequences. In order to test whether tissue-level expression is correlated with chromosome-specific paralog duplication, *Disholcaspis cinerosa* target genes were matched to *B. kinseyi* paralogs using gene tree topology (S4). Transcriptome sequences were assigned to CynOG (S5) by hmmsearch. Reads were quantified against genome-derived transcript sets or Trinity assemblies with Salmon v1.10.3 (Patro et al. 2017). Expression values are given as transcripts per million (TPM), normalized to the total transcripts per sample (Table S2b).

A total of 14 Trinity assemblies were generated (Table S1), with a wide range of read counts and assembly qualities (protein BUSCO completeness 36.5-93.4%). Transcriptomes were only used to contribute to gene phylogenies and not to support claims about chromosome position or gene loss, so we did not apply a minimum coverage threshold. Read-sorting identified two larval samples with predominant hits to chalcidoid genomes, which were removed from analysis. Three kinds of cynipid larvae are found within Cynipini galls, including the Cynipini inducer and *Ceroptres* and *Synergus* inquilines. Read-sorting allowed us to construct three *Synergus* assemblies (from galls of *Belonocnema kinseyi,* *Druon quercuslanigerum,* and *Druon ignotum*) and one *Ceroptres* assembly (from galls of *Druon ignotum)*. Trinity assemblies and predicted proteins were generated for Synergini: *Synergus* ex *Belonocnema kinseyi, Synergus* ex *Druon ignotum*, *Synergus* ex *Druon quercuslanigerum*; Ceroptresini: *Ceroptres* ex *Druon ignotum;* and Cynipini: *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides, Disholcaspis cinerosa, Neuroterus howertoni*, *Andricus quercusfoliatus,* *Druon ignotum, Bassettia pallida, Dryocosmus quercuspalustris*, *Biorhiza pallida*, *Neuroterus numismalis,* and *Neuroterus quercusbaccarum.*

## S3. Gene family identification and outgroup context 

To identify all members of our target gene families, we scanned all predicted proteins against the dbCAN HMM database (dbCAN-HMMdb-V10; Yin et al 2012\) at the subfamily level (dbCAN-sub v5.2.4; Zheng et al 2023\) using HMMER3.4 hmmsearch (E-value \< 10^-10) to assign CAZy classifications and domain boundaries (Cantarel et al. 2009). Subfamily assignments in each cynipoid PCWDE family were validated by submitting a representative structural pdb from each family to the CAZyme3D database (Siva Shanmugam and Yin, 2025). Structures were predicted with LocalColabFold v1.5.2 using the AlphaFold2-ptm model (Jumper et al. 2021; Mirdita et al. 2022), one model per sequence, three recycles, ranking by pLDDT, and MMseqs2 UniRef/environmental MSAs (--model-type alphafold2\_ptm \--num-models 1 \--num-recycle 3 \--rank plddt \--msa-mode mmseqs2\_uniref\_env; Steinegger and Söding, 2017). Signal peptides were predicted with SignalP v6 and removed before folding where present (Teufel et al. 2022). Full CAZy results were retained, including substrates. After enzyme type information allowed us to predict specific oligosaccharides generated by each PCWDE, we searched the dbCAN-sub listed substrates of other CAZy hits to identify enzymes potentially capable of degrading oligosaccharides further.

During preliminary search, we identified a fourth *B. kinseyi* gene of apparently bacterial origin: a compact B-lectin carbohydrate-binding module. dbCAN HMM search was inadequate to identify all members of this gene family because domain intactness varied and many eukaryotic B-lectin genes exist. To identify only genes of the apparently bacterial B-lectin, we used the membership in the CynOG (S5) containing the *B. kinseyi* gene, including assigned transcripts, then validated that they contained at least the B-lectin domain. For each protein, full domain boundaries were assigned using HMMER3 hmmscan against the Pfam-A database (Mistry et al. 2021\) at conditional E-value \<1e-3. 

Some genomes may contain evidence of bacterial genes too degraded to recover in annotation. To detect these pseudogene fragments, a representative sequence for each gene family was used as a tblastn v2.16.0+ (E-value \< 1e-5, SEG filtering enabled, max 200 targets; Camacho et al. 2009\) query against all 75 assemblies, including retained, alternate, and excluded assemblies. To avoid false positives, tblastn hits were treated as provisional and results were evaluated only for genome-gene family combinations lacking an accepted Helixer/dbCAN family member. For GH5\_2, PL1\_8, and PL4\_4, candidate HSPs were required to overlap the focal query domain by at least 150, 150, and 250 amino acids, respectively, and to have at least 45% amino-acid identity. B-lectin candidates were required to derive from a non-plant query, align for at least 90 amino acids, have a bit score of at least 120, and have at least 40% identity. Finally, HSP findings were queried against CynOGs and hits that didn’t match the corresponding PCWDE gene were removed. We found only one case for which a gene family was represented in a genome only by a pseudogene. *Parnips nigripes* contains a PL1\_8-like region whose tblastn hits fell below the 45% identity threshold, which was characterized separately by protein-to-genome alignment of two cynipid PL1\_8 proteins (*Aulacidea tavakolii* and *Ceroptres masudai*) with miniprot v0.9 and exonerate v2.4.0 (protein2genome), followed by a dbCAN-sub hmmsearch of the translated alignment.

To identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome, outgroup sequences were identified using three methods. First, representative *B. kinseyi* protein sequences from each gene family were used as DIAMOND BLASTp (Buchfink et al. 2021\) queries against the NCBI nr database (E value \<10^-10, query coverage \>50%, up to 1000 hits per query). Where possible, corresponding CDS were found in the nt database by matching accession number or using tblastn. Second, after CAZy subfamily assignments for each gene family were determined, all listed members of those subfamilies were identified on [CAZy.org](http://cazy.org) and downloaded from NCBI using GenBank accession numbers. Finally, an additional set of outgroup matches were found by searching structural prediction pdb files for a single representative gene from the cynipoid set against the CAZyme3D database. Outgroup screens were conducted separately for PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3 and B-lectin.

After deduplication, our outgroup screens identified 306 total PCWDE outgroup sequences (Table S3). For PL1\_8, we found 57 bacterial sequences and 6 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (3 Cecidomyiidae, 2 Apidae, 1 Oomycota). For PL4\_4, we found 64 bacterial sequences and 13 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (6 fungus, 4 *Cuscuta*, 3 Coleoptera). For GH5\_2, we found 154 bacterial sequences and 12 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (9 Nematoda, 2 Protista, 1 Coleoptera). We found 35 bacterial PI-PLC-X sequences. For GH30\_3, we found 122 bacteria and 66 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (40 fungus, 20 metazoa, 5 rotifer, 1 plant). For B-lectin, we found 112 bacteria and 160 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (68 plant, 38 fungus, 36 metazoa, 18 rotifer). However, only one outgroup sequence contained the full intact domain architecture including PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and tandem B-lectin: bacterial species *Actinoplanes awajinensis*.

We found a variety of other eukaryotic sequences in each gene family, not all of which have been treated explicitly in published literature on horizontal transfers (Figure S3). These include a lineage of Nematodes possessing bacterial GH5\_2, two distinct bee acquisitions (Figure S3), one midge acquisition, and one oomycete acquisition of PL1\_8, and acquisitions of PL4\_4 by weevils, *Aspergillus* molds, and the parasitic plant *Cuscuta*.

## S4. Gene-tree construction

Two sets of gene trees were constructed for each gene family. A transfer-context tree including all non-cynipoid outgroups was generated to understand the affinity of each cynipoid copy to those found in bacteria and other eukaryotic lineages. A cynipoid gene tree rooted on a single bacterial outgroup was generated to understand the post-transfer history of each gene. 

Because input sequence composition is heterogeneous due to accessory domains and gene model errors, we repredicted dbCAN subfamily domains at a more permissive E-value (\<10^-5) and trimmed sequences to the dbCAN hmmsearch-predicted target domain plus 50 flanking amino acids. Proteins containing multiple non-overlapping domain hits covering over 30% of the HMM profile were split into independent units. Where dbCAN hits for the same family overlapped on one protein (including hits sharing only an endpoint), only the hit with the lowest E-value was kept. Apparent gene-prediction fusions were excluded only when the protein contained a single partial target domain in an otherwise unrelated ORF, were \>2x the family median length, and their target domain covered \<50% of the protein.

To build alignments for phylogenetic trees, cynipoid and outgroup protein sequences were filtered to remove incomplete or unusually long gene predictions. The final domain-trimmed analysis sets contained 427 GH5\_2 sequences (261 cynipoid, 166 outgroup; median length 318 aa), 365 PL1\_8 sequences (302 cynipoid, 63 outgroup; median 294 aa), and 446 PL4\_4 sequences (367 cynipoid, 79 outgroup; median 555 aa) (Table S3). B-lectin sequences were processed separately to accommodate domain diversity. Of 67 cynipoid B-lectin-family proteins identified, 48 contained at least one intact B-lectin domain. Because some proteins contributed up to four B-lectin domains, and because two domains from the domain-complete representative bacterium *Actinoplanes awajinensis* were included, 69 trimmed B-lectin domains were used for phylogenetic analysis. Catalytic-domain sets were constructed independently and included 204 GH30\_3 (16 cynipoid and 188 outgroup) and 42 PI-PLC-X domains (7 cynipoid and 35 bacterial). 

To generate both sets of phylogenies, sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Maximum-likelihood protein trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (Wong et al. 2026\) under a fixed LG+G4 model. Branch support was estimated with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates (-B 1000; Hoang et al. 2018). Cynipoid locus trees (Figure S5; Figure 4 panels are pruned from them) were inferred from the copies in all 49 panel genomes, using the best-E-value domain-overlap rule, together with transcript tips from Trinity assemblies of *Neuroterus numismalis*, *N. quercusbaccarum,* and *Biorhiza pallida* larvae. Each family's tree was rooted on the bacterial sequence with the smallest mean patristic distance to the cynipoid tips in the transfer-context tree. The final trees contain 255 GH5\_2, 294 PL1\_8 and 358 PL4\_4 cynipoid tips plus that outgroup. Four *Bassettia pallida* transcript-derived sequences (one PL1\_8, two PL4\_4 and one B-lectin) were 99.1–100% identical in aligned protein sequence to *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides* sequences and lay 0.000–0.014 substitutions/site from them, so they were attributed to *C. quercusbatatoides*. They were pruned from the transfer-context and B-lectin trees after inference and were excluded before inference from the locus trees. 

For B-lectin domain proteins, because many contained more than one copy of the domain, tree building was conducted in two stages. First, all B-lectin domains were isolated and a tree was constructed as above (Figure S6). In that tree, each domain was assigned to either a branch containing the N-terminal (D1) or C-terminal (D2) bacterial B-lectin. Subsequently, these two branches were aligned as partitions and analyzed in IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under an edge-proportional linked model (-p). ModelFinder (-m MFP; Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017\) selected HIVB+G4 for D1 and WAG+G4 for D2. Branch support was assessed with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates with BNNI (-B 1000 \-bnni; Hoang et al. 2018\) and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates (-alrt 1000; Guindon et al. 2010).

## S5. Chromosome homology and synteny blocks

Two methods were used to determine whether members of each gene family were found in consistent genomic regions of different species. Our primary method was to use RagTag to align all scaffolds from all non-chromosome scale genomes to the 10 chromosome-scale scaffolds of the GCF\_010883055.1 *B. kinseyi* assembly, numbered by length in decreasing order, as reference (Alonge 2022). For *Euceroptres primus,* which was assembled to 6 chromosome-scale scaffolds, and for *Dryocosmus kuriphilus*, which was assembled to 10 chromosomes (Liu et al. 2024), JCVI v1.5.11 was used to assign target genes to *B. kinseyi*\-chromosome-equivalent regions (Tang et al. 2024). Because reference-guided scaffolding can join fragments across chromosomal boundaries, references to chromosomes 1-10 refer to genomic blocks based on the 10 *B. kinseyi* pseudochromosomes and are used as a coordinate system rather than statements about karyotype.

Our second method was to examine the flanking genes surrounding each target gene. This analysis also allowed us to validate that genes for each target family are integrated into cynipoid genomes and rule out bacterial sequences included due to assembly errors. First, genomes were annotated with Helixer (S2) and masked for repeats. RepeatModeler v2.0.7 was used to create a per-genome library of repetitive elements (Flynn et al. 2020). This library was deduplicated, filtered to remove known genes, and used to guide RepeatMasker v4.2.2 softmasking of all genomes (Smit et al. 2013-2015). An orthology catalog was initially constructed from 667,137 proteins from 41 genome proteomes after excluding proteins for which at least 30% of CDS bases were repeat-masked. Proteins were assigned to orthogroups, designated CynOGs, with OrthoFinder v2.5.5 in orthogroups-only mode (-og), using DIAMOND for sequence search (-S diamond; Buchfink et al. 2021), 32 search and analysis threads (-t 32 \-a 32), and MCL inflation 1.5 (van Dongen 2000). After merging co-linear gene-model fragments, proteins with at least 15% repeat overlap were removed from orthogroups represented in five or fewer genomes; orthogroups left without members were discarded. This produced 22,448 CynOGs containing 623,796 proteins. Profile HMMs were constructed for CynOGs containing at least three members. Sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto; Katoh and Standley 2013), trimmed with trimAl (-automated1; Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009), and converted to profile HMMs with HMMER 3.4 hmmbuild (Eddy 2011), yielding 17,928 profiles. A self-recovery check using hmmsearch assigned 99 of 100 randomly selected source proteins to the correct CynOG, and the profile library was indexed with hmmpress. Proteins from nine later-added or replacement proteomes as well as proteins predicted from transcriptomes were searched against this library at E \< 1 × 10⁻⁵ and assigned to the CynOG with the best full-sequence E-value and score.

Because genome size and intergenic distance vary substantially within Cynipoidea, synteny was evaluated using gene order rather than physical distance. For each genome, we therefore created an index listing CynOG assignments in their order of appearance along each pseudochromosome. To verify homology between paralogs, flanking gene context around annotated target genes was used to define synteny blocks (tblastn-only loci were excluded). Synteny blocks were identified by counting flanking genes at windows of 5, 15, and 50 on either side of each target gene, excluding tandem duplicates of the target itself. Pairwise synteny between two loci of a given family was assessed using the Jaccard index of their flanking CynOG signatures. Locus pairs with Jaccard ≥0.15 at any window size were considered syntenic. Synteny blocks were identified by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on the narrow-window (±5 genes) Jaccard distance matrix (1 − J), cutting the dendrogram at distance 0.85 (corresponding to J \= 0.15). Because this threshold requires a relatively small number of shared genes, it is common for loci to be assigned connections to multiple neighbors that themselves share no flanking genes. This transitive chaining can link wholly unrelated genome regions as false synteny blocks. Average linkage mitigates this problem by requiring each potential match to resemble all members of an existing block. Confidence tiers of synteny blocks were assigned based on which window sizes supported the pair: high (detected at ±5 genes), medium (±15 genes only), or low (±50 genes only).

After shared synteny blocks were defined, we looked for each conserved flanking gene pattern across all 49 genomes. We filtered the flanking gene set to only those present in more than 50% of block members and looked for occurrences clustered within 500 kb. After a synteny block was identified, we rescanned all gene models within the search region for target hits, including tblastn hits. When a match was discovered, the genome contained a target hit at that locus, and the number of tandem repeats was counted; if it didn’t, the locus was marked EMPTY. If fewer than 3 conserved flanking genes could be found in a cluster, the block was marked NOT\_DETERMINED for that genome.

We found 77 flanking gene patterns could be matched across more than one genome (Table S5). Of these, 26 contained target genes in two or more genomes, accounting for 68.5% (463/676) of genome-derived target loci. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 10 was recovered in 38/49 genomes; 26 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 12 were empty. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 45/49 genomes; 22 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 23 were empty. The modal PL1\_8 block on chromosome 8 was recovered in 43/49 genomes, including 27 with PL1\_8 and 16 empty. The modal PL4\_4 block on chromosome 7 was recovered in 37/49 genomes, including 27 with PL4\_4 and 10 empty. The modal B-lectin block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 45/49 genomes, including 17 with B-lectin and 28 empty.

## S7. Introns and GC3 composition of transferred genes

To assess transformation of genes after insertion into wasp genomes, we evaluated intron placement and nucleotide frequency. Intron positions were extracted from gene model predictions for each target hit in each genomic reference in which they were found. Introns were defined as gaps between consecutive CDS features. For each family, a single *B. kinseyi* reference protein was selected. All other genomic homologs were aligned to that reference with Biopython's PairwiseAligner (BLOSUM62, gap open −10, gap extend −0.5, global mode; Cock et al. 2009). Each intron was assigned an amino acid position relative to the coordinates of that reference sequence. Intron placements were clustered at a maximum distance of three amino acids. For each intron placement cluster, the dominant splice phase, splice signal, and chromosomal distribution of genes with that intron site were recorded. Flanking context was extracted for each modal site to verify that introns were found at consistent placements within ungapped, unambiguous alignment regions. 

To obtain a baseline rate of intron placement, we conducted a second scan using distinct methods. For each gene, the transcript with the longest CDS was used, and transcripts whose CDS features spanned more than one scaffold or strand were excluded. Introns were defined as gaps between consecutive CDS features, as for the PCWDE copies. Because background genes share no common reference protein, intron positions were not aligned or clustered; instead, each intron was assigned a relative position equal to the fraction of the CDS preceding it, measured in nucleotides from the start codon. The same method was applied to the PCWDE introns for comparison. The background comprised 2,911,604 introns in 649,754 genes and was summarized as the fraction of introns in the first quarter of the CDS and the median relative position. Target-family genes (631) were not removed from the background. This baseline places 32.4% of all introns in the first quarter of their gene, while 85.1% of PCWDE introns fall in the first quarter of their gene. 

Nucleotide frequency was assessed only at the third codon position (GC3), because this site is more likely to be synonymous and therefore faces reduced stabilizing selection. For each genome, we calculated a background GC3 distribution from the longest coding sequence of every annotated gene. GC3 was measured for 603 PCWDE copies and 28 B-lectin copies, and each copy was ranked by its percentile of the background rate in its genome. Using the transfer-context tree (S4), we chose the closest sampled bacterial relatives of the cynipoid members of each gene family and evaluated their GC3. For each genome and gene family, we calculated the median absolute difference in percentage points between wasp copies and the genome background median, and between bacterial relatives and the genome background median. The two values were compared across species with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (37, 37 and 36 species for GH5\_2, PL1\_8 and PL4\_4; 16 for B-lectin; Table S8). Wasp copies lay within 2.6–4.4 percentage points of their host-genome GC3 median, versus 16.7–19.2 points for their closest bacterial relatives (Wilcoxon signed-rank P \< 10⁻¹⁰ for each family; B-lectin 11 vs 57 points, P \= 3 × 10⁻⁵).

## S8. Catalytic site intactness and changes due to selection

After insertion into eukaryotic genomes, bacterial genes are exposed to new selection pressures and may experience losses of catalytic capacity, altered binding site geometry, or changed surface chemistry. To confirm that each protein retained the experimentally determined catalytic residues for its family, sequence alignment was used to identify catalytic amino acid positions. Catalytic residues were defined from experimentally characterized family members. GH5\_2 positions were referenced to *Bacillus agaradherans* Cel5A (PDB 1A3H, chain A), in which E139 is the catalytic acid/base and E228 is the nucleophile (Davies et al. 1998). The conserved PL1 catalytic arginine was referenced to R236 of *Aspergillus niger* pectin lyase B (PDB 1QCX, chain A; Vitali et al. 1998), corresponding to the homologous R236 experimentally demonstrated to be essential for catalysis in *A. niger* pectin lyase A (Sánchez-Torres et al. 2003). PL4\_4 positions were referenced to K150 and H210 of *Aspergillus aculeatus* rhamnogalacturonan lyase (PDB 1NKG, chain A; McDonough et al. 2004), which are proposed to act as the proton abstractor and proton donor, respectively (Jensen et al. 2010). Reference sequences and cynipoid domain sequences were aligned de novo with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 1). Each mapped position was classified as containing the expected reference residue, a substitution, or a gap/ambiguous residue. Gaps and ambiguous positions were reported separately and excluded from percentages calculated among observable sites (Table S10). Substitutions at catalytic residues were mapped onto gene trees. The catalytic arginine was conserved in all PL1\_8 sequences. In GH5\_2 and PL4\_4, substitutions generally occurred in isolated tips. Two sister PL4\_4 sequences, from *Diplolepis spinosa* and *Pediaspis aceris*, lacked the expected proton-donor histidine, carrying phenylalanine and serine, respectively.

To generate codon alignments for selection analyses, we retrieved the coding sequence corresponding to each retained cynipoid protein. A coding sequence was included only when its translation exactly reproduced the protein sequence used in the family alignment. Protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 8), and coding sequences were threaded onto the protein alignments. Codon columns containing gaps in more than 50% of sequences were removed. Maximum-likelihood trees were inferred from the filtered codon alignments with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under GTR+G4 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000 \-T 16 \--safe; Hoang et al. 2018).

To identify branches with evidence of episodic diversifying selection within Cynipoidea, we used aBSREL (Smith et al. 2015\) in HyPhy v2.5.85 (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020\) with \--branches All. Each branch was tested independently for a proportion of sites with omega greater than 1, and significance was assessed at the HyPhy Holm-corrected p \< 0.05 threshold.

Because bacterial outgroups are too divergent for reliable codon-level comparison, per-site selection analysis was restricted to cynipoid sequences. FUBAR (Murrell et al. 2013\) was run in HyPhy v2.5.85 with default parameters. Sites were classified as under purifying or diversifying selection at posterior probability \> 0.9; all other retained sites were classified as neutral for the summary.
