# Abstract

Lateral gene transfer from bacteria can enable dramatic shifts in animal resource use by delivering enzymes already adapted for a biochemical function. One such shift occurred in cynipoid wasps, one of the few lineages of the primarily entomophagous apocritan wasps to have transitioned to herbivory. Plant cell walls pose particular digestive challenges for apocritan larvae because their blind midgut prevents passage of indigestible fiber as frass. Building on prior work identifying three cynipoid plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (PCWDEs) as bacterially derived, we use a comparative genomic approach to evaluate the contribution of these enzymes to the evolution of herbivory in Cynipoidea. Analysis of 49 genomes, including a new genome for *Euceroptres primus*, supports independent transfers of three PCWDEs near the crown of Cynipoidea, approximately 159–195 million years ago. All three genes are retained in herbivorous, gall-associated lineages in Cynipidae and Diplolepididae, but broadly absent in the entomophagous family Figitidae. Within oak gall wasps, PCWDEs are highly expressed in larval midguts, where enzymes likely aid digestion by solubilizing cell wall polymers. Duplications produced additional paralogs expressed in larval salivary glands and adult venom glands, indicating secondary recruitment into plant interactions potentially implicated in gall induction. Finally, we recover an unreported fourth bacterial transfer of a complex multidomain protein expressed in oak gall wasps as a B-lectin domain. This evidence demonstrates that bacterial gene transfer supplied key molecular tools enabling herbivory and helped establish the ecological context in which gall induction could evolve.

# Introduction

Plants are one of the most abundant biological resources on Earth, but their tissues present substantial nutritional and defensive challenges to consumers (Futuyma & Agrawal 2009; Behmer 2009; Mithöfer & Boland 2012). They are often low in nutrient density, high in fiber, and can contain toxic defensive compounds. In spite of this, herbivory has evolved repeatedly in animals, demonstrating the strong ecological opportunity offered by this abundant resource (Futuyma & Agrawal 2009). Herbivorous insects have developed a wide variety of solutions to these problems, including microbial symbioses, detoxification mechanisms, and behavioral specialization on particular plant tissues (Hansen & Moran 2014; Després et al. 2007; Tooker & Giron 2020). 

Among insects, microbial associations are a common solution to the challenges of herbivory. Digestive endosymbionts can aid their hosts (e.g., termites, aphids, beetles) by detoxifying defensive compounds, synthesizing necessary nutrients, or degrading otherwise inaccessible plant substrates (Engel and Moran 2013; Kucuk, 2020). In some cases, bacterial functions have been acquired permanently by a eukaryotic genome through lateral gene transfer. These genes often originate from obligate reproductive or digestive endosymbionts, although in many cases no extant symbiont can be identified (Wybouw et al. 2016). A large proportion of such bacterial transfers to insects involve plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (PCWDEs), including bacterial mannanases, xylanases, pectinases, and cellulase-like enzymes that help insects process otherwise inaccessible plant polysaccharides (Acuña et al. 2012; Pauchet and Heckel, 2013; Kirsch et al. 2014; Shelomi et al. 2014; Wybouw et al. 2016). Lineage-scale studies of stick insects and cerambycid beetles have shown that these transfers have preceded radiations into herbivorous niches and that transferred genes subsequently diversified through duplication, regulatory divergence, or changes in substrate specificity (Shelomi et al. 2016; Shin et al. 2022).

Hymenoptera (bees, ants, and wasps) have an interesting evolutionary history with respect to herbivory. While extant members of the group are primarily entomophagous, ancestral hymenopterans were likely herbivores resembling modern-day sawflies (Blaimer et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025), which pass undigested plant material as frass. The group transitioned to entomophagous parasitoidism more than 200 million years ago near the origin of the suborder Apocrita (Zhang et al. 2025). Apocritan larvae are characterized by a blind midgut, which is not connected to the hindgut. Food enters the midgut, where enzymatic digestion occurs. While soluble nutrients are absorbed through the gut lining, indigestible residues accumulate in the midgut until they are expelled as a meconium before pupation (Quicke 1997; Bağrıaçık 2020). The blind midgut presents an additional obstacle to any potential transition back to consuming plants. Without the capacity to pass frass, insoluble cell wall polymers ingested by chewing plant tissue would accumulate in the midgut, limiting total intake volume. While several transitions back to herbivory have occurred in Apocrita, all of them are associated with specialized feeding strategies (Tooker and Giron 2020). 

While some Apocritan herbivores mitigate digestive challenges by feeding on existing plant resources with high nutrient density like nectar and pollen (bees) or seeds (seed-feeding ants), gall wasps elicit novel resources. Gall wasps co-opt the development of their host plants to induce and control development of a parasite-specific organ called a gall. Gall wasp larvae then feed on specialized nutritive tissue lining a central larval chamber (Bronner 1992; Brooks and Shorthouse 1998; Stone et al. 2002; Stone and Schönrogge 2003). Larvae can feed for months, and during this time they remain confined to their larval chamber and cannot expel indigestible material. Nutritive cells are enriched in nutrients and largely lack secondary cell walls (Bronner 1992; Brooks and Shorthouse 1998), but their cell walls still contain cellulose and pectin (Cosgrove 2005). Cleavage of these polysaccharides could reduce accumulation of indigestible material in the larval midgut (Hearn et al. 2019).

Digestive endosymbionts are not unknown in Apocrita, but are primarily facultative, and have not been documented in Cynipoidea (Brucker and Bordenstein 2012). However, laterally transferred PCWDEs have been observed in cynipid wasps (Cambier et al. 2019; Hearn et al. 2019). Cynipid gall-forming wasps comprise one of the most structurally complex and phenotypically variable groups of gall inducers. Cynipids are diverse and distributed worldwide, with over 1,400 described species (Ronquist et al. 2015; Buffington et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2026). All known members of the family are gall inhabitants, but within that context, multiple feeding strategies are found, including multiple independently-evolved lineages of gall inducers, multiple lineages of herbivorous gall inquilines, and some lineages that may be at least partly entomophagous (Blair 1949). The largest tribe, Cynipini, is entirely composed of gall inducers, contains more than 1,000 described species, and is primarily associated with oaks (family Fagaceae). Many Cynipini with known life cycles alternate between the sexual generation, containing males and females, and the asexual generation, containing only females. Each generation typically induces a distinct gall. Hearn et al. (2019) reported expression of three classes of PCWDEs in sexual generation feeding larvae of *Biorhiza pallida*: rhamnogalacturonan lyase, pectin/pectate lyase, and cellulase. Subsequent work reported expression of all three enzymes across larval development in *Diplolepis rosae* as well (Mozhaitseva et al. 2023). A preliminary survey of available genomes suggested that PCWDE acquisition occurred early in the evolutionary history of cynipoids and that copy numbers of each gene vary across lineages (Hearn et al. 2019). 

As putative digestive enzymes, the primary expected context for insect expression of PCWDEs would be in the larval gut. Surprisingly, however, high expression of an enzyme in Cynipini labeled β-1,4-endoglucanase, indicating the potential capacity to degrade cellulose, was first observed in the adult female venom gland of *Biorhiza pallida* (Cambier et al. 2019). This gene expression is associated with direct biochemical evidence of both cellulolytic and pectinolytic activity on *B. pallida* eggs (Bronner 1973). Such evidence suggests that cynipid PCWDEs have important host-interaction functions beyond larval digestion. In plants, β-1,4-glucanases and pectate lyase-like enzymes are expressed to facilitate tissue remodeling (Notaguchi et al. 2020; Ye et al. 2025). Expression of these endogenous enzymes is induced by physical wounding (Ikeuchi et al. 2022), and exogenous glucanase application enhances callus formation and graft survival (Kawakatsu et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2024). The functional applications of PCWDEs in Cynipini may be more diverse than direct digestion alone. 

The transfer, retention, expression, and expansion of PCWDEs indicate that they play an important role in cynipoid wasp biology, possibly facilitating both herbivory and gall induction. To evaluate this history, we tested two sets of predictions. If each PCWDE family derives from a single ancient bacterial transfer, cynipoid copies should form a monophyletic lineage broadly consistent with known patterns of descent, and many genes should share chromosome context or intron positions. Ecologically, if these genes contributed to the transition to herbivory, their retention and expansion should be concentrated in herbivorous, gall-associated lineages, catalytic residues should be conserved, and expression should be concentrated in tissues associated with digestion or plant interaction. We examine 48 cynipoid genomes including a new chromosome-scale assembly for *Euceroptres primus*, an early-diverging member of the entomophagous family Figitidae and one of the few sampled figitids still associated with galls, and one outgroup, *Telenomus remus*. We also analyze RNA-seq data from whole larvae of 14 cynipid species, larval tissues of two Cynipini species, and adult tissues of seven Cynipini species. Finally, we identify a previously undescribed bacterially derived gene family, originally acquired as a multidomain protein containing endo-β-1,6-glucanase and B-lectin domains but retained primarily as a non-catalytic B-lectin. Together, these analyses document the genomic domestication of bacterial carbohydrate-associated genes and clarify how an ancestrally entomophagous lineage acquired a molecular toolkit for consuming and manipulating plant tissue. 

# Results

## A chromosome-scale genome was generated for *Euceroptres primus*

A new chromosome-scale genome assembly was generated for the figitid species *Euceroptres primus.* Preliminary work showed that this was the only sampled figitid to retain PCWDEs. Confident resolution of the chromosomal context of each gene in this species provides important evidence about the timing and placement of the original lateral transfer events.

PacBio HiFi sequencing yielded a highly contiguous genome for *Euceroptres primus*. From one Revio cell where the barcoded *E. primus* SMRTBell library represented one third of the sequencing run, 2,706,303 HiFi reads (37.95 Gb of HiFi data) were obtained, of which 30 (0.0011% of reads) were discarded after filtering with HiFiAdapterFilt. K-mer analysis of the final assembly relative to the PacBio HiFi reads used to create the contig assemblies reported raw and QV scores of 65.867 and 63.392 respectively. The genome coverage was estimated at 44.6× coverage as reported by GenomeScope2.

The final genome consisted of 15 scaffolds totaling 839 MB, an N50 of 155 MB, L50 of 3, N90 of 107 MB, and L90 of 6 (Figure S5). The assembly has a fairly complete hymenoptera\_odb12 BUSCO, C:97.4% \[S: 96.5%, D:0.9%\], F:0.8%, M:1.8% (n \= 5,920). Hi-C scaffolding revealed six chromosomes (Figure S5) with only 8 contigs representing 970 Kb (0.12%) of the genome remaining unplaced, and the genome was mostly homozygous. EGAPx annotated 14,633 genes, including 12,973 protein-coding genes and 1,737 lncRNA.

## 49 genomes in four families passed quality control

We considered 70 genomes for our core superfamily analysis, of which 12 were removed because they fell below a 40% BUSCO completeness threshold (Table S1). Taxonomic redundancy (8) and other assembly quality issues (1) prompted the removal of another 9 genomes, resulting in a core analytic set of 49 annotated genomes, including the Platygastroid outgroup *T. remus*, 3 Diplolepididae (rose and maple gall inducers), 11 Figitidae (entomophagous parasitoids), and 34 Cynipidae (herb, bramble, and oak gall inducing and inquiline wasps) (Table S1). The genome of *Cecinothofagus ibarrai* was available but fell below the quality threshold (15.3% genome BUSCO completeness), so the basal cynipoid family Paraulacidae is not represented.

## Trinity assemblies were generated for 14 species

Genomic data were supplemented with RNA-seq reads from a wide variety of sample types, from whole larvae or adults to dissected tissues to whole galls containing small larvae, which were assembled de novo as needed and quantified against genomic or de novo protein predictions (Table S1 lists genome, de novo assembly, and RNA-seq expression availability per species). Of the 14 transcriptome assemblies, four were generated from previously published RNA-seq datasets and ten from new datasets presented here for the first time. We obtained a wide range of read counts and assembly qualities (protein BUSCO completeness 36.5-93.4%). Transcriptomes were only used to contribute to gene phylogenies and not make claims about chromosome position or gene loss, so we did not apply a minimum coverage threshold. Read-sorting identified two larval samples with predominant hits to chalcidoid genomes, which were removed from analysis. Three kinds of Cynipid larvae are found within Cynipini galls, including the Cynipini inducer and *Ceroptres* and *Synergus* inquilines. Read-sorting allowed us to construct three *Synergus* assemblies (from galls of *Belonocnema kinseyi,* *Druon quercuslanigerum,* and *Druon ignotum*) and one *Ceroptres* assembly (from galls of *Druon ignotum)*. Kraken2 screening identified only two bacterial symbionts: *Wolbachia* spp. (majority of samples) and *Rickettsia* sp. (1 sample). A total of 14 Trinity assemblies and predicted proteins were generated: Synergini: *Synergus* ex *Belonocnema kinseyi, Synergus* ex *Druon ignotum*, *Synergus* ex *Druon quercuslanigerum*; Ceroptresini: *Ceroptres* ex *Druon ignotum;* and Cynipini: *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides, Disholcaspis cinerosa, Neuroterus howertoni*, *Andricus quercusfoliatus,* *Druon ignotum, Bassettia pallida, Dryocosmus quercuspalustris*, *Biorhiza pallida*, *Neuroterus numismalis,* and *Neuroterus quercusbaccarum.* 

## Genes belong to known CAZyme subfamilies 

Cynipoid members of each putative PCWDE family were screened against the dbCAN and CAZyme3D databases to match them to known catalytic classes and infer plausible enzyme activity. Sequences and structures were consistently assigned to three CAZy subfamilies: (1) GH5\_2, (2) PL1\_8, or (3) PL4\_4. (1) Genes annotated as “Endoglucanase z-like” were assigned to CAZy subfamily GH5\_2. This subfamily contains EC 3.2.1.4 cellulase activity. Its known substrates are β-1,4 glucan polymers including cellulose, and it generates short soluble cellodextrin β-glucans as byproducts via endo-hydrolysis of β-1,4 bonds. GH1\_e72, an enzyme with EC 3.2.1.21 β-glucosidase activity, was identified as a candidate degrader of short soluble β-D-glucans via terminal cleavage, though dbCAN-sub does not list cellodextrin specifically as a substrate. This enzyme was found in all cynipoid species. (2) Genes annotated as “Pectin lyase” were assigned to PL1\_8, which is listed to include EC 4.2.2.10 pectin lyase activity. This enzyme cleaves homogalacturonan, specifically methyl-esterified α-D-galacturonan via β-elimination, producing unsaturated methyl-esterified oligogalacturonides. (3) Genes annotated as “Rhamnogalacturonan lyase” were assigned to PL4\_4, which is listed to have EC 4.2.2.23 rhamnogalacturonate endolyase activity. It performs endo type eliminative cleavage of rhamnogalacturonan I backbone linkages, generating unsaturated rhamnogalacturonate oligosaccharides. Our substrate search of the dbCAN-sub listings found no enzymes known to target pectin oligosaccharides in cynipoids. 

A fourth bacterially derived gene was also discovered during the course of this study and found in all Cynipid tribes except Eschatocerini and Synergini. The CynOG originally identified due to a *B. kinseyi* gene containing a bacterial B-lectin gene was discovered to also include paralogs with a more complex domain composition. In its most complete form, this gene includes from N to C terminus domains annotated as PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and a tandem pair of B-lectin domains. These were identified as Pfam codes PF00388, PF02055, and PF01453, respectively. The PI-PLC annotation indicates homology to the catalytic fold of phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipases C. Unlike eukaryotic PI-PLC enzymes, which require paired X and Y components (Essen et al. 1996), bacterial PI-PLC proteins cleave phosphatidylinositol as single-domain units (Heinz et al. 1995, 1998). The GH30\_3 domain corresponds to EC 3.2.1.75, glucan endo-1,6-β-glucosidase, which hydrolyzes β-1,6 linkages in β-1,6-glucans and β-1,6-oligoglucosides. The B-lectin domains are consistent with D-mannose-binding lectin domains of the bulb-type lectin family.

## Outgroup matches were found in diverse bacterial and eukaryotic groups

Outgroup sequences were screened in the NCBI nr, CAZy, and CAZyme3D databases to identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome. After deduplication, our outgroup screens identified 308 total PCWDE outgroup sequences (Table S2). For PL1\_8, we found 57 bacterial sequences and 6 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (3 Cecidomyiidae, 2 Apidae, 1 Oomycota). For PL4\_4, we found 65 bacterial sequences and 14 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (6 fungus, 4 *Cuscuta*, 4 Coleoptera). For GH5\_2, we found 154 bacterial sequences and 12 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (9 Nematoda, 2 Protista, 1 Coleoptera). Because we discovered an additional putatively bacterial domain in the full B-lectin gene family, outgroup screens were conducted separately for PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3 and B-lectin. We found 35 bacterial PI-PLC-X sequences. For GH30\_3, we found 122 bacteria and 66 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (40 fungus, 20 metazoa, 5 rotifer, 1 plant). For B-lectin, we found 112 bacteria and 158 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (66 plant, 38 fungus, 36 metazoa, 18 rotifer). However, only one outgroup sequence contained the full intact domain architecture including PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and tandem B-lectin: bacterial species *Actinoplanes awajinensis*. 

Our outgroup search for each gene recovered a variety of eukaryotic representatives in addition to a large bacterial contingent. The pattern of gene presence and absence across the tree of life provides strong support for their acquisition through lateral gene transfer. GH5\_2 and PL4\_4 are completely absent from the immediate phylogenetic neighbors of Cynipoidea, including all hymenopteran and dipteran references (Figure 1). One PCWDE family, PL1\_8, is present in *Contarinia nasturtii* (Swede midge)*, Habropoda laboriosa* (southeastern blueberry bee)*,* and *Ceratina calcarata* (spurred small carpenter bee), but absent in other Cecidomyiid and Apid genomes. B-lectin is found in all cynipid tribes except Eschatocerini and Synergini. For contrast, we show the gene count of ancestrally present eukaryotic glycoside hydrolase gene GH1\_e72, which is present nearly universally and varies only in copy number.

## PCWDEs were obtained from various bacteria, retained by gall-associated wasps

With the exception of the *B. kinseyi* reference which retained the NCBI annotation, genomes were uniformly annotated with Helixer and screened for bacterially transferred gene family members. To confirm gene absence, an additional scan using tblastn was also conducted. Of the 48 cynipoid genomes in our filtered set, PCWDEs are found in the gall-associated clades of Cynipoidea and are generally absent in the figitid clade that transitioned to a parasitoid lifestyle. GH5\_2 is present in 34 genomes, PL1\_8 in 35, PL4\_4 in 34, and B-lectin in 23 (Table S2). The gall-inducing family Diplolepididae possesses all three PCWDEs with the exception of *Pediaspis aceris*, which lacks GH5\_2 (Figure 1). In Figitidae, the gall-inhabiting parasitoid *Euceroptres primus* retains PL1\_8 and GH5\_2, but lacks PL4\_4. All other species, which are free-living entomophagous parasitoids, lack intact copies of all three genes. A pseudogenized copy of PL1\_8 was found in *Parnips nigripes*, containing multiple frameshifts and internal stops. Cynipids include multiple galling and inquiline lineages, and all of them retain all three PCWDEs, though in two cases evidence is weak. In herb galler *Eubothrus centaureae*, PL1\_8 is detected by tblastn but not annotated by Helixer. In inquiline *Ceroptres kovalevi,* PL4\_4 is absent in both annotation and tblastn, but the Trinity assembly of congener *Ceroptres* ex *Druon ignotum* does contain this gene. None of our target genes were found in Paraulacidae (sister to the rest of Cynipoidea), although the low genome BUSCO completeness (15.3%) of the only representative of that family leaves this result inconclusive. By contrast, the B-lectin gene was found only in Cynipidae. Further, detection of this gene by Helixer was less consistent, and many more occurrences were contributed only by tblastn. Within Cynipidae, the gene was present in Phanacini, Qwaqwaini, Diastrophini, Ceroptresini, Aylacini, and Cynipini, but absent in *Aulacidea* and Synergini. This retention and loss pattern does not correspond to any obvious difference in life history.

To build alignments for phylogenetic trees, cynipoid and outgroup protein sequences were filtered to remove incomplete or unusually long gene predictions. The final domain-trimmed analysis sets contained 427 GH5\_2 sequences (261 cynipoid, 166 outgroup; median length 318 aa), 365 PL1\_8 sequences (302 cynipoid, 63 outgroup; median 294 aa), and 446 PL4\_4 sequences (367 cynipoid, 79 outgroup; median 555 aa) (Table S2). B-lectin sequences were processed separately to accommodate domain diversity. Of 67 cynipoid B-lectin-family proteins identified, 48 contained at least one intact B-lectin domain. Because some proteins contributed up to four B-lectin domains, and because two domains from the domain-complete representative bacterium *Actinoplanes awajinensis* were included, 69 trimmed B-lectin domains were used for phylogenetic analysis. Catalytic-domain sets were constructed independently and included 195 GH30\_3 (7 cynipoid and 188 outgroup) and 37 PI-PLC-X domains (2 cynipoid and 35 bacterial).

Phylogenetic trees for GH5\_2 and PL1\_8 place the cynipoid lineages as directly sister to bacterial-dominated sister lineages (100% and 82% UFBoot, respectively), with other eukaryotic outgroup genes scattered throughout the tree (Figures 2 and S1). In PL4\_4, the closest siblings of the cynipoid sequences are found in beetles (98% UFBoot), but both are siblings to a single bacterial sequence from *Erwinia tracheiphila*, a plant pathogen (99% UFBoot). In PL1\_8, the cynipoid sequences are sister to a larger lineage dominated by phytopathogenic Pectinobacteriaceae (*Dickeya, Brenneria, Pectobacterium*) and Erwiniaceae (*Erwinia, Pantoea*). In GH5\_2, cynipoid sequences fall in a lineage primarily composed of marine Gammaproteobacteria (*Microbulbifer*) and soil-associated Betaproteobacteria (*Massilia, Eleftheria*). Most bacterial neighbors of cynipoid GH5\_2 proteins include a pair of CBM6 domains. These accessory domains are absent in all cynipoid members of this family, suggesting a domain loss occurred during or shortly after transfer. Independent trees for PI-PLC and GH30 recover different neighbors (Figure S2). The siblings of PI-PLC are composed almost entirely of *Actinoplanes* spp., while GH30 siblings include *Actinoplanes,* a variety of other Actinomycetes, and *Terriglobus roseus*, all broadly known to be soil bacteria. Aside from heterogeneous exceptions known from marine contexts, all closely related bacterial outgroups for each of the four gene families are found in soil and plant-associated contexts. They are not lineages known to be insect endosymbionts.

## Each gene has one or two modal chromosome placements

We conducted chromosome-scale synteny comparisons to identify the original insertion site and track patterns of duplications and translocations of each gene across Cynipoidea. The majority of cynipoid target proteins were successfully assigned to *B. kinseyi* chromosomes by RagTag or JCVI contig alignment (Table S3): 80.4% of GH5\_2 proteins were placed (119/148), 81.6% of PL1\_8 (160/196), and 73.9% of PL4\_4 (133/180). Of 46 genome-derived B-lectin-family loci, 28 (60.9%) were placed. Based on these chromosome assignments, each gene family has one or two clear modal regions in Cynipidae (Figure 1). Across cynipid genomes, PL1\_8 is found predominantly on regions equivalent to *B. kinseyi* chromosome 8, PL4\_4 on chromosome 7, GH5\_2 on chromosomes 6 and 10, and B-lectin on chromosomes 3 and 6\. Placements are more varied in families more distant from Cynipini, but the chromosome 8 placement of PL1\_8 is shared across all families, and the chromosome 7 placement of PL4\_4 is shared in Diplolepididae. These placements are consistent with a single original insertion at those regions in the common ancestor of the three families. The original placement of GH5\_2 cannot be resolved from this analysis. 

B-lectin is found with its full domain structure intact on chromosomes 6 in nearly all cynipid tribes, indicating this is the original placement. An additional placement on chromosome 3 is limited to Cynipini and contains only one intact B-lectin domain. A number of translocations to nonmodal positions occurred in each family; see Figure 1\. *Eschatocerus acaciae,* a cynipoid with inconsistent placement in prior published phylogenies but placed as basal to Cynipidae in Hearn et al. (2024), shares the modal cynipid chromosome placements for the three PCWDEs but lacks the bacterial B-lectin. This is congruent with its published placement in Cynipidae, but suggests the acquisition of B-lectin occurred after its divergence.   
   
To identify true orthologous loci within each family and to verify the absence of genes in lineages where the target proteins weren’t found, we compared flanking gene sequences adjacent to each occurrence of each gene to identify blocks of conserved synteny. A total of 84 flanking gene patterns could be matched across more than one genome (Table S4). Of these, 27 contained target genes in two or more genomes, accounting for 79.8% (455/570) of genome-derived target loci. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 10 was recovered in 37/49 genomes; 26 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 11 were empty. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 44/49 genomes; 22 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 22 were empty. The modal PL1\_8 block on chromosome 8 was recovered in 42/49 genomes, including 27 with PL1\_8 and 15 empty. The modal PL4\_4 block on chromosome 7 was recovered in 36/49 genomes, including 28 with PL4\_4 and eight empty. The modal B-lectin block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 40/49 genomes, including 13 with B-lectin and 27 empty. 

Target genes in many synteny loci occur in tandem duplications, up to a maximum of 10 per locus. In many cases, synteny blocks recovered by our criteria as independent are found in relatively close proximity; six PL1\_8 blocks were separated by fewer than 20 genes. Synteny blocks were also detected in species lacking target genes, marked as absent in Table S4, giving strong evidence that absence of the gene in those species indicates a block predates insertion or postdates loss, rather than poor assembly quality. Species taxonomically closer to *B. kinseyi* recovered more flanking genes per locus and synteny blocks were less likely to be undetectable even in poor quality assemblies. In the most distant species, the outgroup Platygastroid *T. remus*, only 24/77 cross-genome-detected synteny blocks were recovered, all of which lacked the target protein, supporting the inference that the transfer happened after the divergence of Cynipoidea and Platygastroidea.

## PCWDE gene structures share conserved intron placements

Introns are strong evidence that a gene is incorporated into the cynipoid genome and provide an independent line of evidence to infer when and how many times each transfer occurred. All three PCWDE families contain introns (Figure 3; Table S5), and placement relative to CDS is conserved within each PCWDE family, indicating that all candidate proteins from each family were the result of a single transfer and later duplications. Modal intron positions are shared across paralogs regardless of chromosome placement. Among proteins with coherent single-scaffold CDS models, 87.1% (155/178) of GH5\_2 paralogs have an intron at reference amino acid position 18, including 55 placed on chromosome 6 and 48 on chromosome 10\. 41.0% (87/212) of PL1\_8 paralogs contain an intron at reference amino acid position 9\. 38.6% (83/215) of PL4\_4 paralogs have an intron at reference amino acid position 83, and an additional 15.3% (33/215) have one at position 37\. In each case, the modal intron is found in all cynipoid families where the gene is present. Introns in all three PCWDEs are strongly biased toward the N terminus: 84.6% of introns were observed within the first quarter of the CDS, compared with 34% for all genes. The median relative position of PCWDE introns was 5.5% of the way through the CDS; the genome-wide median was 43.3%. Intron density remains lower than host background rate, with median values in PCWDEs around 1 intron per kb of CDS compared to 4 per kb across all gene types. 

Intron placement was less informative for B-lectin genes. Only 13% (3/23) of genome-derived B-lectin genes contain any introns, and only one intron position is shared in two genomes. However, incomplete gene models due to inconsistent annotation by Helixer may limit intron discovery, and the loss of the majority of the gene in its chromosome 3 duplication prevents the use of intron placement to independently validate its homology with the chromosome 6 occurrence.

## Horizontally transferred genes take on nucleotide frequencies of wasp genomes

Bacterial and eukaryotic genomes have different baseline nucleotide frequencies, and transferred nucleotide sequences are expected to become more similar to the context of the recipient. While coding bases are constrained by selection, the third base in each codon can vary because changes there are often synonymous. Therefore, the proportion of GC at the third codon (GC3) can indicate a shift from bacterial baseline to eukaryotic baseline. Within bacteria, all three PCWDE gene families show widely varied rates of GC3 %, with medians above 50%. Wasp genes have a much lower baseline GC3 frequency, with a median of 38%. After acquisition by Cynipoids, all three gene families moved toward the wasp genome baseline. The closest bacterial relatives in each gene tree would be in the 95th-98th 95–98th host percentile of wasp GC3 richness. Wasp PCWDE copies are 2.6 / 4.3 / 3.0 percentage points from the wasp median vs 19.1 / 19.3 / 16.7 for bacterial relatives. 

## Gene phylogenies show paralog duplication within each family

Per family gene phylogenies (Figures 4 and S3) show that repeated phylogenetically deep duplication events have occurred in all three PCWDE families. The placement of GH5\_2 and PL1\_8 sequences from *Diplolepis rosae*, *Euceroptres primus*, and *Eschatocerus acaciae*—taxa external or early-diverging within Cynipidae—among otherwise cynipid lineages suggests that some of these duplications occurred early in cynipoid evolution. By contrast, PL4\_4 shows extensive duplication only after the divergence of *Eschatocerus* from the remainder of Cynipidae. Within Cynipidae, branches independently recapitulate all or large parts of the phylogeny of the family. Most deviations from the accepted phylogeny are weakly supported. In GH5\_2, three such deep duplications exist on chromosome 6 (Figure S3). Proteins on chromosome 10 have also duplicated, but patterns are more consistent with recent divergences within Synergini and Cynipini. PL4\_4 and PL1\_8 each show five duplications ancestral to Cynipidae and at least one more within Cynipini, all primarily localized on their modal chromosomes 7 and 8 respectively but also including a number of translocations to other regions. Paralog proliferation of all three PCWDEs is ancient and ongoing.

## PCWDE Paralogs show tissue-specific expression

Quantification of RNA-seq data shows that high PCWDE paralog count created by gene duplication is associated with expression in multiple secretory tissues (Table S6). Whole-body larval data show that several paralogs of each gene family are expressed in each Cynipid species sampled, including Cynipini gall inducers and *Ceroptres* and *Synergus* inquilines (Figure 4). Tissue-scale data from gall-inducers *D. cinerosa* and *B. kinseyi* larval tissue-level expression reveal that within each larva, distinct paralogs peak in gut or salivary glands (Figure 6). Expression in adult venom glands was only sampled in Cynipini, and varied considerably between species (Table S6). Across all tissues, PL1\_8 expression peaked in larval salivary glands, where it was the 33rd and 35th most expressed gene in *D. cinerosa* and *B. kinseyi* respectively. The gene also had secondary expression in the gut and in adult venom in *D. cinerosa* but not *B. kinseyi*. PL4\_4 is overwhelmingly specific to the gut (55th in *D. cinerosa*, 4th in *B. kinseyi*), with minor expression in salivary gland and Malpighian tubules. Across both species, GH5\_2 is expressed at 83,000-131,000 TPM in larval gut samples (the most expressed gene in both species), 6,000-52,000 TPM in salivary glands (7th and 1st in *D. cinerosa* and *B. kinseyi*), and 1,700-7,600 TPM in adult venom glands (22nd and 41st). In each gene family, *B. kinseyi* paralogs show extreme tissue specificity, and *D. cinerosa* genes highest in each tissue are recovered as siblings to the corresponding protein in *B. kinseyi* (Figure 4), indicating that recruitment of particular paralogs to specific tissue contexts has been a stable process across the evolution of Cynipini. The modest (300-800 TPM) and gut-focused expression of GH1\_e72, an ancestral eukaryotic CAZyme, highlights the exceptional expression of the bacterial PCWDEs in gut, salivary gland, and venom gland.

## PCWDEs retain catalytic residues

To determine if the target proteins experienced losses of catalytic capacity or novel selection pressures after transfer to a eukaryotic genome, we examined catalytic site conservation and several metrics of selection. Coding sequences were recovered for 257 of 260 GH5\_2 proteins (98.8%), 300 of 302 PL1\_8 proteins (99.3%), and 354 of 365 PL4\_4 proteins (97.0%). After removal of gap-rich codon columns, the alignments retained 15.6%, 12%, and 12.8% of codon sites, respectively (Table S7). Across families, 64-75.7% of retained codon sites showed evidence of purifying selection, and median omega (rate of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution) ranged from 0.300 to 0.427, indicating most selection was purifying. FUBAR, which tests per-site selection across all branches, identified 3-8 sites per family (0.5-2.4%) with evidence of pervasive diversifying selection. aBSREL, which tests episodic selection across whole structures on particular branches, identified episodic diversifying selection on 12 of 257 GH5\_2, 26 of 300 PL1\_8, and 24 of 354 PL4\_4 terminal branches (4.7-8.7%; Table S7). Catalytic residues were strongly conserved. The expected residue occurred in 246 of 249 GH5\_2 sequences at the acid/base position (98.8%) and 233 of 237 at the nucleophile position (98.3%), all 285 PL1\_8 sequences at the catalytic-arginine position (100%), and 268 of 277 and 262 of 270 PL4\_4 sequences at the proton-abstractor and proton-donor positions, respectively (96.8% and 97.0%; Table S7). Losses of catalytic residues were not concentrated in any single clade of the gene tree for any family. Collectively, these results show that stabilizing selection has acted strongly to retain the structures needed for catalytic activity in all three enzyme families, including catalytic sites and overall protein structure.

# Discussion

Our combined evidence from synteny, intron position, and phylogeny supports three principal conclusions. First, Cynipoidea acquired three bacterial PCWDEs before the divergence of Figitidae, Diplolepididae, and Cynipidae and a B-lectin before divergence within Cynipidae (Figure 1). Second, these genes were vertically inherited after acquisition, with subsequent apparent losses in figitid lineages (Figure 1). Third, their retention and duplication in gall-inducing lineages produced a conserved enzymatic toolkit for solubilizing cell wall polysaccharides, which could be deployed in multiple tissues and contexts (Figures 4, 6).

We find strong evidence of four LGT events in which three PCWDEs and one multi-domain B-lectin protein absent in related insects were transferred from bacteria to Cynipoidea. These PCWDEs are present in both Diplolepididae and Cynipidae (gall inducers and inquilines) and subsequently lost in the majority of Figitidae (entomophagous parasitoids). Shared flanking genes, chromosome regions, and modal intron placements across all three PCWDEs in Diplolepididae, *Euceroptres primus*, and Cynipidae, are all consistent with a single insertion event per gene, suggesting absences are the result of gene loss events (Figures 1 and 3, Table S4). Following phylogenies from Blaimer et al. (2020) and Hearn et al. (2024), this indicates that a single transfer event for each of the three PCWDEs occurred before the divergence of these three families, dated by Blaimer et al. to between 159.1–160.2 mya (Blaimer et al. 2023; Hearn et al. 2024). Given the incompleteness of the available assembly for Paraulacidae, we cannot exclude the possibility that one or more of these transfers occurred at the crown of Cynipoidea (190.5–195 mya), so we conservatively place all three events in the 159–195 mya range. The bacterial B-lectin is present in all cynipid tribes excluding Eschatocerini and Synergini, indicating it was acquired around the crown of Cynipidae, a lineage Blaimer et al. (2020) date to 71.6–141.7 mya. These events indicate that ancient Cynipoids were repeatedly exposed to bacterial genes, some of which were subsequently retained across millions of years of evolution. 

## Role of PCWDEs in digestion of plant tissue

That cynipoids acquired three PCWDE genes within a relatively short span of evolutionary time suggests that their ancestral ecological context provided both exposure to bacteria carrying PCWDEs and a selective advantage for retaining them. According to ancestral state reconstructions by Blaimer et al. (2020), when cynipoids acquired these bacterial PCWDEs, they were not gall inducers. Rather, they were either endophytic parasitoids (or potentially hyperparasitoids) of an herbivorous insect, or an inquiline associated with galls induced by another insect. In either case, it is likely that the ancestor of modern cynipoids oviposited into plant tissue. In that context, a transition to herbivory would have offered immediate access to an abundant food source. However, high fiber content would have posed an obstacle to larvae exploiting this resource. Laterally transferred genes are a proven solution to this problem (Acuña 2012; Pauchet & Heckel 2013; Kirsch 2022), and cynipoids deploy this solution in triplicate to solubilize cellulose, homogalacturonan, and rhamnogalacturonan I simultaneously. 

Because cell wall polymers are made of sugars, enzymes that cleave them could theoretically make these sugars available as an energy source. However, each PCWDE cleaves internal bonds in polymers and generates oligomers of at least two sugars that require further degradation by specialized enzymes to become absorbable sugars (Davies et al. 1998; Jensen et al. 2010; Kirsch et al. 2016). Plausible cynipoid enzymes for pectin oligosaccharide degradation were not found in our CAZyme search. However, there is one candidate that may be capable of further degradation of cellulose. GH5\_2 endo-cleavage of cellulose produces cellodextrins (Davies et al. 1998), and our CAZyme search identified one native cynipid protein that has the potential to further digest cellodextrins. GH1\_e72 enzymes catalyze the terminal cleavage of β-D-glucosyl residues, which is the broad class of enzyme activity necessary to degrade cellodextrins (Kim et al. 2022). However, the actual substrate specificity of the cynipoid paralogs cannot be determined without experimental assays. Further, GH1\_e72 was expressed in Cynipini gut tissue only at around 4–600 TPM. This expression level is low relative to the over 100,000 TPM expression of GH5\_2. The low expression and uncertain substrate of GH1\_e72, as well as the apparent absence of pectin oligomer degrading enzymes, suggests that gut-expressed PCWDEs are not a major pathway for converting cell wall polysaccharides into absorbable sugars. In this respect, Cynipoidea resemble the ancestral hymenopteran herbivores in clade Symphyta, which also lack enzymatic pathways that would allow them to obtain energy from plant cell wall polymers (Fogal and Webb 1976; Figure 1).   
   
Even when their breakdown products cannot be metabolized, cleaving cell wall polymers can confer substantial digestive benefits. In the herbivorous beetle *Phaedon cochleariae*, knockout of fungal-derived GH28 polygalacturonases impaired larval growth and survival, which were rescued by supplementation of enzyme but not oligomer. This suggests that pectin degradation is primarily advantageous for its indirect effects on absorption of other nutrients, not for the energetic contents of the pectin polymer (Kirsch et al. 2022). Work in mammals suggests that this may be due to increased viscosity of gut contents caused by soluble pectins, which has been shown to delay starch digestion and glucose absorption (Bai and Gilbert 2022). Cellulose presents a different challenge. As a large component of cell wall biomass, it can significantly dilute nutritionally usable dietary intake. The specific mechanism by which cellulose impedes larval development is not known. In caterpillars, dietary dilution with cellulose reduces growth, an effect they are able to partially mitigate through compensatory feeding (Lee 2017). In Apocrita, compensatory feeding is limited by the inability to pass frass. Intake of higher volumes of tissue directly entails retention of more indigestible material. For gall wasps, this problem is substantially mitigated before consumption begins. Herbivorous cynipoids live in galls and feed on nutritive tissue or gall cortex tissue. Nutritive tissue is composed of a unique cell type distinguished by endoreduplicating nuclei and nucleoli (Bronner 1977; Harper et al. 2004; Jankiewicz et al. 2021), intense protein synthesis (Bronner 1977; Rohfritsch 1975; Jauffret 1973), accumulation of fat and sugar, but suppression of starch, crystallized cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin synthesis (Bronner 1977; Ferreira and Isaías 2013). Despite their high nutritional density and palatability, nutritive tissue cells retain primary cell wall synthesis during cell division (Bronner 1977; Markel et al. 2024). In early phases, Cynipini larvae may feed on fluid in larval chambers before mechanically piercing cells (Markel et al. 2024). Later stages use mandibles to open cells physically, and cell wall polymers are likely prevalent in the imbibed liquid (Bronner 1992; Schonrogge et al. 2000). Therefore, while gall wasps benefit from a nutritionally enriched diet, they still face challenges imposed by fiber accumulation. Due to the structure of their digestive system, these challenges cannot be overcome by removal as frass. Enzymatic cleavage does not remove sugars from apocritan guts, but transforming them from polymers to oligomers may be sufficient to mitigate their negative effects.

While the precise mechanisms of PCWDE function and impact on digestion and survival are challenging to investigate and difficult to generalize, their acquisition from microbes is a frequent precursor to radiations of herbivorous lineages in insects (Kirsch et al. 2014; Shelomi et al. 2016; McKenna et al. 2019; Shin et al. 2022). Acquisition of microbial glycoside hydrolases occurred early in the histories of several major herbivorous insect clades, including GH28 in Euphasmatodea and Phytophaga, GH5\_2 in Cerambycidae, and GH5\_40 in Cicadellidae (Kirsch et al. 2014; Shelomi et al. 2016; McKenna et al. 2019; Shin et al. 2022; Molligan et al. 2026). We infer that the acquisition of PCWDEs had similarly profound ecological and evolutionary consequences for cynipoids. Most related wasp lineages are entirely entomophagous. Their digestive anatomy limits total larval food intake, and they lack enzymes to digest plant-derived polymers. Within a single interval, Cynipoidea acquired enzymes targeting three distinct polysaccharides, dramatically increasing their metabolic capacity to handle plant cell-walls. By solubilizing these polymers and reducing their capacity to obstruct diffusion, PCWDEs could have significantly relaxed the constraint imposed by blind midgut anatomy and facilitated the shift to herbivory, a necessary precondition for the evolution of the capacity to induce galls, which subsequently evolved at least once and potentially multiple times across Diplolepididae and Cynipidae (Blaimer et al. 2020).

## PCWDEs were lost in lineages not associated with galls

While the majority of modern cynipoids are either inquilines or inducers of galls, the majority of Figitid species (\~1,700) are entomophagous parasitoids with no association with galls or plants in general. The retention of PL1\_8 and GH5\_2 in an early branching Figitid, *Euceroptres primus*, supported by the pseudogene of PL1\_8 in *Parnips nigripes*, indicates that these genes were present in the ancestral members of this family and lost at least twice. PL4\_4 was lost at least once, possibly at the root of the family. The retention of PCWDEs in some members of Figitidae is closely linked with their life history. Dissection and micro-CT work (Brown et al. 2026\) has demonstrated that *Euceroptres* *primus* is a parasitoid and feeds directly on gall-inducer larvae. Despite this shift to entomophagy, *E. primus* retains two of the three bacterial PCWDEs. This suggests that cell wall polymers may remain digestively relevant for gall-inhabiting entomophages. Such parasitoids may encounter cell wall polymers either in prey gut contents or in direct or incidental ingestion of nutritive cells during feeding. By contrast, the majority of parasitoids in Figitidae do not feed within plant tissue, and many of their host insects are not herbivores. Free-living entomophagous parasitoids therefore likely experience relaxed selection to retain PCWDEs, allowing their subsequent loss. 

## PCWDE paralogs show tissue-specific expression

The consistency with which gall-associated lineages retained all three genes underlines the importance of these genes in that context. Subsequent duplications indicate that they are versatile tools that have found additional applications. Cynipini, which have the largest genomes in Cynipoidea and likely in Hymenoptera more generally, show especially extensive gene duplications, both as tandem arrays and as translocations to other chromosomes (Figure 1). Prior experimental work in Cerambycidae has shown that GH5\_2 enzymes can diversify to target xylan, xyloglucan, cellulose, and mannan (Shin et al. 2022). In Cynipini, enzymes retain residues consistent with catalytic activity, but substrate specificity remains undetermined. Instead, the clear distinction between paralogs is in their tissue-specific expression. Gut expression of GH5\_2 is substantial, representing more than 10% of protein-coding expression in that tissue, consistent with a role solubilizing abundant polymers. However, other paralogs of GH5\_2 are highly expressed in larval salivary gland or adult venom gland. The most highly expressed paralogs of PL1\_8 are in larval salivary glands. PL4\_4 peaks in the gut but other paralogs are most highly expressed in larval salivary glands or Malpighian tubules. Larval salivary gland expression of all three PCWDE families has also been reported in *Diplolepis* *rosae* (Mozhaitseva et al. 2023), indicating that deployment of these genes outside the gut is not restricted to Cynipini. Both venom and salivary expression may be explicable through predigestive functions, loosening cell adhesion and softening cell walls before larval feeding. However, venom is applied many months before larval feeding in some species, so digestion is unlikely to be the entire role of venom expression. Additionally, cell-wall fragments are a known developmental signal in plants, so digestion may not be the only function of these tissue-specific paralogs. 

While PCWDEs are used antagonistically by herbivores to degrade the structure of plant tissue, similar enzymes are also deployed by plants to remodel and repair, which may give us clues to their expression in organs outside of the gut. Physically wounded plants express GH9B β-1,4-glucanases to facilitate cell-wall reconstruction (Notaguchi et al. 2020). This gene expression is part of a wound-repair program (Ikeuchi et al. 2022), and also includes pectate lyase-like enzymes, which directly enable cell expansion (Ye et al. 2025). Exogenous addition of glucanase to wound sites facilitates grafting success (Yang et al. 2024), in part by improving cell-cell adhesion (Kawakatsu et al. 2020). Further, exogenous addition of cellulase and pectinase enzymes in combination with physical wounding has been shown to directly trigger changes in plant gene expression associated with callus formation and vascular regeneration (Zhang et al. 2022). Exogenous addition of pectin-derived oligogalacturonides has been shown to induce wound healing, shoot formation, and flower formation (Lu et al. 2021; Falasca et al. 2008; Marfà et al. 1991). Therefore, prior work shows that PCWDEs similar to those expressed in cynipoid venom and saliva may elicit plant remodeling both through physical loosening of cell wall and through perception of cell-wall fragments generated by these enzymes.

Substrate specificities and signalling pathways in cynipoids may differ, but this research offers a set of clear testable hypotheses about the roles these enzymes play in Cynipini non-gut expression. Venom-applied GH5\_2 β-1,4-glucanases and PL1\_8 pectin lyases could facilitate tissue lysis known to form cavities around cynipoid eggs (Bronner 1973; Shorthouse et al. 2005), promote cell adhesion during wound repair to seal eggs inside, or initiate changes in gene expression that make the tissue more competent for gall induction. Salivary expression of the same pair of PCWDEs may contribute to the gall’s continual renewal of the feeding interface despite ongoing larval feeding damage. More specifically, they may aid in the maturation of nutritive tissue cells, which show thinning cell walls (Hough, 1953\) and expanded cell volume as they differentiate from parenchyma (Harper et al. 2004). In combination, these possibilities suggest that the acquisition of PCWDEs from bacteria offered cynipoids not only the capacity to digest plant material, but also a new set of handles to modify the structure and developmental trajectory of living plant cells. 

## Acquisition of a multi-domain B-lectin protein and subsequent domain loss

While the fourth bacterially transferred gene does contain a glycoside hydrolase domain, it does not fit clearly in the PCWDE category. Its inferred ancestral form contained four distinct domains: a membrane-lipid-cleaving phospholipase, an endo-β-1,6-glucanase, and two bulb-type B-lectins. Domain architecture evidence suggests it was acquired near the crown of family Cynipidae likely from a soil or plant-associated bacterium, potentially an actinomycete. The known targets of GH30\_3 enzymes are β-1,6-glucans, which are typical components of fungal cell walls (Temple et al. 2017). In the outer wall of many fungi, β-1,6-glucan polysaccharides connect mannoproteins to the underlying β-1,3-glucan and chitin scaffold, and their cleavage can affect the structural integrity of the cell wall (Kollár et al. 1997; Wang et al. 2024). Characterized members of the bulb-type B-lectins have been shown to bind mannose-rich glycans like those found in yeast mannan (Kaku et al. 1992), so tandem B-lectins may complement this enzyme’s function by localizing it to mannoproteins. The relevance of the PI-PLC domain remains unclear. Therefore, while the molecular and ecological targets of this enzyme can only be discussed speculatively, the combination of domains present suggests an enzyme that localizes to mannose-rich fungal surfaces and cleaves structural β-1,6-glucan. 

Such a function would be consistent with the known ecology of a possible actinomycete donor. Actinomycete strains selected for antagonism toward glucan-walled microorganisms commonly produce extracellular glucanases capable of cell-wall hydrolysis and lysis (Valois et al. 1996; Fayad et al. 2001). Fungal interactions are also a relevant ecological concern for gall-associated wasps. Endophytic fungi are known to colonize cynipid galls (Wilson 1995\) and chamber-invading fungi can cause substantial larval mortality in gall inducers (Cooper and Rieske 2010). Fungal cell wall degradation is therefore ecologically plausible in both the bacterial donor and the recipient wasp. Tests of functional activity through binding assays and fungal growth inhibition assays, as well as differential expression in the presence and absence of fungal infection of galls, would help clarify the role of this gene. 

The subsequent history of retention and expression of this gene provides little support for a conserved antifungal function. The multidomain form was retained intact in Ceroptresini, Diastrophini, and Cynipini, but appears to have been lost entirely in Synergini. *Ceroptres* larvae express the domain-complete form, but with limited replication and comparative sampling, this is difficult to interpret. Fungal ecology has been studied in too few cynipid systems to make predictions about its retention as a putative antifungal gene across tribes. A fungal cell wall cleavage explanation is further complicated by the observation that in Cynipini, duplication and domain remodeling produced a second paralog retaining only the B-lectin carbohydrate-binding domain. All detectable expression in our Cynipini tissue datasets was associated with this noncatalytic paralog. Any explanation of the functional role of this gene in Cynipini ecology must account for its continued utility in the absence of catalytic activity. Nonetheless, repeated losses, domain reduction, and modest, tissue-variable expression provide an informative contrast to the broad retention, repeated expansion, and high expression of all three PCWDE families. 

## Ecological context and unresolved mechanism of bacterial transfer

Confident identification of specific bacterial donors in ancient LGT events is challenged both by subsequent LGT among bacteria and by extinction of donor lineages (Szollosi et al. 2013). We find several consistent candidate donor groups at higher taxonomic scales, but consider the 159-195 million years elapsed since inferred transfer to make species-level donor identification unreliable. For all four transferred gene families, the bacterial sequences closest to cynipoid sequences belong to lineages known to be associated with soil and plant tissue, including Erwiniaceae, Pectobacteriaceae, Oxalobacteraceae, Cellvibrionales, and Actinoplanes. Given the taxonomic diversity of candidate donors, it is unlikely that all four laterally transferred genes, or even all three PCWDEs, originated from a single donor lineage. Instead, the common feature across donor lineages is the shared ecological context of soil and plant tissue. 

While the importance of bacterial LGT as a mechanism for enabling life history transitions in insects is now well established, the mechanism by which such genes enter insect genomes is unknown. Many insects host germline infections like *Wolbachia*, which offer direct opportunities for heritable genetic exchange. However, the candidate donor lineages are not known to be germline endosymbionts. This is consistent with the pattern observed in other bacterially derived PCWDEs in insects. In the best-resolved cases, inferred bacterial donors are plant-associated or polysaccharide-degrading bacteria rather than obligate insect symbionts: *Streptomyces*\-like actinobacteria for a coffee-berry-borer GH10 xylanase (Padilla-Hurtado et al. 2012), *Cellvibrio*/*Teredinibacter*\-like Gammaproteobacteria for *Phaedon* GH11 xylanases (Pauchet & Heckel 2013), and gut- or food-associated gammaproteobacteria for phasmatodean GH28 polygalacturonases (Shelomi et al. 2016\)*.* On the grounds that cynipoids were ancestrally endophytic parasitoids or inquilines, we hypothesize that the most likely ecological context for gene transfer was exposure of an egg to bacteria during oviposition into nonsterile plant tissues containing insect hosts. How bacterial DNA entered and became heritably inserted in germline chromosomes remains unresolved.

## Conclusion

Our results reconstruct an ancient and recurring history of gene transfer from environmental bacteria to cynipoid wasps, confirming this lineage as possessing an exceptionally diverse repertoire of bacterially acquired PCWDE families in Hymenoptera. Cynipoids acquired and retained three complementary enzyme families with predicted activities against cellulose and the two major pectic backbones, homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan-I. Because the larval midgut is disconnected from the hindgut, undigested plant material cannot be expelled as frass during feeding, potentially constraining dietary intake through the accumulation of refractory cell wall polysaccharides. By facilitating the breakdown of these polymers, the acquired PCWDEs may have alleviated this constraint. These acquisitions preceded the diversification of herbivorous cynipoids into one of the few species-rich herbivorous lineages in Apocrita, including major radiations of gall-inducing wasps. Our comparative dataset documents the post-transfer history of these genes, showing a pattern of tandem duplication and chromosomal translocation in gall-associated lineages and losses in entomophagous lineages. In modern Cynipini, PCWDEs represent a major component of digestive gene expression. Distinct paralogs are also expressed in salivary and venom glands, where they interact directly with living plant tissue and may have acquired novel functions related to gall induction. A fourth bacterial gene, originally encoding a complex protein with multiple catalytic domains, was acquired later and retained as a noncatalytic B-lectin protein. This contrasting outcome demonstrates how horizontally acquired genes can undergo substantial functional transformation. The exceptional preservation and prominent digestive expression of PCWDEs suggest their sustained importance to cynipoid herbivory.

# Materials and Methods

## Genome structure and annotation

We generated a chromosomal-scale genome of *Euceroptres primus* following the protocol outlined in Zhang et al (2026). Briefly, the samples were reared from 53 galls on *Quercus bicolor* in Pennsylvania, 2025\. A single female wasp was homogenized and used for high-molecular-weight DNA extraction, sheared to \~18 Kb, and prepared into a PacBio SMRTbell library with barcoded adapters, then sequenced on a PacBio Revio system; concurrently, a pool of three females was used for to prepare a Hi-C library using the enzymes DdeI and DpnII and was subsequently sequenced on an Element AVITI system. HiFi reads were filtered using FCS-Adaptor and HiFiAdapterFilt (Sim et al. 2022), assembled with HiFiASM (Cheng et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022\) using the insect telomere motif (Vitkova et al. 2005), purged of duplicates with PurgeDups (Guan et al. 2020), and scaffolded with Hi-C reads via BWAmem2 (Vasimuddin et al. 2019), Picard (Broad Institute 2019), and YaHS (Zhou et al. 2023), with manual editing in Juicebox (Durand et al. 2016); contig coverage was assessed with Minimap2 (Li 2018), completeness with BUSCO (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025\) using hymenoptera\_odb12, and taxonomic assignments with BLAST+ (Camacho et al. 2009), Diamond (Buchfink et al. 2021), and FCS-GX (Astashyn et al. 2024), summarized with Blobtools2 (Challis et al. 2020\) and blobblurb, to remove non-arthropod and Wolbachia contigs. A transcriptome from a single female was sequenced, with reads filtered by Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014\) and expression quantified with Salmon (Patro et al. 2017). Genome annotation was performed using EGAPx with STAR, Miniprot, and Gnomon, supplemented by BRAKER3 (Gabriel et al. 2024\) with RepeatModeler and OrthoDB v12, while genomic elements were derived with bedtools (Quinlan and Hall 2010\) and Pfam domains assigned via HMMER (Eddy 2011; Mistry et al. 2021). 

A total of 70 genomes were considered for inclusion (Table S1). The candidate panel was composed of 69 Cynipoid genomes and one outgroup, *Telenomus remus* (Platygastroidea: Scelionidae). In addition to the new *Euceroptres primus* genome, a set of 41 cynipoid assemblies and *Te. remus* were downloaded from NCBI in October 2025\. Additionally, 19 sets of whole-genome DNA reads were downloaded from ENA and assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 (Bankevich et al. 2012). Finally, an additional 9 assemblies were downloaded from NCBI in July 2026\. Of these, we chose *Belonocnema kinseyi* (NCBI accession GCF\_010883055.1) as our reference genome. This accession was originally uploaded as *Belonocnema treatae*, before the Texas population from which the samples were collected was re-described as *B. kinseyi* (Zhang et al. 2021). In order to verify the absence of each gene family in the ancestors of Cynipoidea, we also downloaded 15 protein sets for a representative panel of Hymenoptera and Diptera. These were used for whole genome protein detection only and excluded from chromosome-scale analysis. BUSCO v6.0 was used to evaluate the completeness of genomes relative to the hymenoptera\_odb12 protein set (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025). To prevent bias in our analyses from incomplete genomes, we set a 40% minimum completeness threshold to retain the most complete available genomes representing each cynipoid lineage. 

The majority of prior evidence from genomes assembled to chromosome scale (*T. remus,* *Dr. kuriphilus, B. kinseyi;* Xu et al 2021, Liu et al 2024\) and from direct karyotyping (Gokhman et al 2011, 2015\) have found that the modal chromosome count in cynipids is n=10, which is likely ancestral among Platygastroidea and Cynipoidea (Gokhman 2009). However, haploid chromosome count is known to vary among cynipoids (e.g., *Leptopilina boulardi* n=9; *Leptopilina clavipes* n=5; *Eubothrus scabiosae* n=9; *Andricus mukaigawae* n=6; *Andricus kashiwaphilus* n=5) (Abe 1998, Gokhman et al 2011, 2015). Only a single figitid species, *Callaspidia defonscolombei,* has been documented with more than 10 (n=11, Gokhman et al 2011). To assign comparable spatial coordinates despite varying chromosome number, we used RagTag to align all non-chromosome scale genomes to the 10 chromosome-scale scaffolds of the GCF\_010883055.1 *B. kinseyi* assembly, numbered by length in decreasing order, as reference (Alonge 2022). For *Euceroptres primus,* which was assembled to 6 chromosome-scale scaffolds, JCVI v1.5.11 was used to assign target genes to *B. kinseyi*\-chromosome-equivalent regions (Tang et al. 2024). Because reference-guided scaffolding can join fragments across chromosomal boundaries, subsequent references to chromosomes 1-10 refer to genomic blocks based on the 10 *B. kinseyi* pseudochromosomes and are used as a coordinate system rather than statements about karyotype. 

Since only three of the genomes (*B. kinseyi*, *Leptopilina boulardi* and *L. heterotoma*) were previously annotated, Helixer v0.3.6 (model invertebrate\_v0.3\_m\_0100; Stiehler et al. 2021\) was used to predict genomic elements. NCBI annotations were retained only for *B. kinseyi*. 

## Ortholog catalog and synteny

To identify orthologous loci within each family, we compared flanking gene sequences adjacent to each occurrence of each gene. This analysis also allowed us to validate that genes for each target family are integrated into cynipoid genomes and rule out bacterial sequences included due to assembly errors. Synteny detection was complicated by the recurrence of repetitive Transposable Elements (TEs) among predicted gene elements. To mitigate that problem, RepeatModeler v2.0.7 was used to create a per-genome library of repetitive elements (Flynn et al. 2020). This library was deduplicated, filtered to remove known genes, and used to guide RepeatMasker v4.2.2 softmasking of all genomes (Smit et al. 2013-2015). The final 49-genome panel contained 920,372 predicted proteins. An orthology catalog was initially constructed from 667,137 proteins from 41 genome proteomes after excluding proteins for which at least 30% of CDS bases were repeat-masked. Proteins were assigned to orthogroups, designated CynOGs, with OrthoFinder v2.5.5 in orthogroups-only mode (-og), using DIAMOND for sequence search (-S diamond; Buchfink et al. 2021), 32 search and analysis threads (-t 32 \-a 32), and MCL inflation 1.5 (van Dongen 2000). After merging co-linear gene-model fragments, proteins with at least 15% repeat overlap were removed from orthogroups represented in five or fewer genomes; orthogroups left without members were discarded. This produced 22,448 CynOGs containing 623,796 proteins.

Profile HMMs were constructed for CynOGs containing at least three members. Sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto; Katoh and Standley 2013), trimmed with trimAl (-automated1; Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009), and converted to profile HMMs with HMMER 3.4 hmmbuild (Eddy 2011), yielding 17,928 profiles. A self-recovery check using hmmsearch assigned 99 of 100 randomly selected source proteins to the correct CynOG, and the profile library was indexed with hmmpress. Proteins from nine later-added or replacement proteomes and proteins predicted from transcriptomes were searched against this library at E \< 1 × 10⁻⁵ and assigned to the CynOG with the best full-sequence E-value and score.

Because genome size and intergenic distance vary substantially within Cynipoidea, synteny was evaluated using gene order rather than physical distance. For each genome, we therefore created an index listing CynOG assignments in their order of appearance along each pseudochromosome. To verify homology between paralogs, flanking gene context was used to define synteny blocks. Synteny blocks were identified by counting flanking genes at windows of 5, 15, and 50 on either side of each target gene, excluding tandem duplicates of the target itself. Pairwise synteny between two loci of a given family was assessed using the Jaccard index of their flanking CynOG signatures. Locus pairs with Jaccard ≥0.15 at any window size were considered syntenic. Synteny blocks were identified by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on the narrow-window (±5 genes) Jaccard distance matrix (1 − J), cutting the dendrogram at distance 0.85 (corresponding to J \= 0.15). Because this threshold requires a relatively small number of shared genes, it is common for loci to be assigned connections to multiple neighbors that themselves share no flanking genes. This transitive chaining can link wholly unrelated genome regions as false synteny blocks. Average linkage mitigates this problem by requiring each potential match to resemble all members of an existing block. Confidence tiers of synteny blocks were assigned based on which window sizes supported the pair: high (detected at ±5 genes), medium (±15 genes only), or low (±50 genes only).

After shared synteny blocks were defined, we looked for each conserved flanking gene pattern across all 49 genomes. We filtered the flanking gene set to only those present in more than 50% of block members and looked for occurrences clustered within 500 kb. After a synteny block was identified, we rescanned all gene models within the search region for target hits. When a match was discovered, the genome contained a target hit at that locus, and the number of tandem repeats was counted; if it didn’t, the locus was marked EMPTY. If fewer than 3 conserved flanking genes could be found in a cluster, the block was marked NOT\_DETERMINED for that genome. 

## Transcript data

Transcriptome data was used to provide physiological and ecological context of gene expression as well as supplement the taxonomic coverage of our dataset, albeit without genomic locus information. To determine if genes were expressed in specific tissues we produced RNA-seq data for larval gut, salivary glands, and Malpighian tubules of two species and adult venom and accessory glands of seven species. We additionally sequenced whole body larvae and adult carcasses (whole body minus venom and accessory glands). In Cynipini, accessory glands produce fluid that coats eggs during oviposition, while venom glands produce fluid injected into host plants surrounding the egg (Guiguet et al. 2023). For whole body larvae, whole asexual galls were collected of *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides, Disholcaspis cinerosa, Neuroterus howertoni*, *Druon quercuslanigerum, Andricus quercusfoliatus* (fall 2024, Houston, TX), *B. kinseyi* (fall 2025, Houston, TX), and *Druon ignotum* (September 2025, Baxter, MN). Larvae were dissected out of fresh galls. For venom expression, asexual galls of *Disholcaspis cinerosa, Bassettia pallida, Callirhytis quercusbatatoides,* and *Neuroterus howertoni* and sexual generation galls of *B. kinseyi* were collected for adult rearing. Shortly after emergence, venom glands and accessory glands were dissected out in 1x PBS using microforceps. Venom glands, accessory glands, and carcass for RNA-seq were collected in fall 2024-winter 2025\. Tissue-specific larval RNA-seq (gut, salivary glands, head, cuticle, and malpighian tubules) were collected in fall 2025 from asexual galls of *Disholcaspis cinerosa* and in winter 2026 from sexual galls of *B. kinseyi*. RNA extraction was conducted using the PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Library prep and RNA-seq were conducted by Novogene. Additional publicly available RNA-seq reads were obtained from SRA for *Dryocosmus quercuspalustris* (whole larvae; Martinson et al. 2022), *Biorhiza pallida* (whole larvae in galls, Hearn et al. 2019; adult venom and ovary tissue; Cambier et al.2019), and *Neuroterus numismalis* and *Neuroterus quercusbaccarum* (whole larvae in galls, Bellows et al. 2024). 

Reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014\) and assembled per species using Trinity (Grabherr et al. 2011\) for species for which genomes were unavailable. Trinity assemblies were postprocessed with EviGene (Gilbert 2013\) to reduce redundancy and TransDecoder (Haas et al. 2013\) to predict amino acid sequences. Reads were sorted against a panel of hymenopteran and oak genomes with BBSplit (BBMap v39.37; Bushnell 2014), using minratio=0.56, minhits=1, and ambiguous2=best, to identify parasitoid and inquiline wasp contamination, which is common among wild gall wasps. Samples containing insect reads predominantly contributed by non-inducer species were split. Chalcidoid parasitoid samples were discarded, while cynipid inquiline samples were retained, assembled, and included to increase phylogenetic coverage. Kraken2 screening was conducted on larval reads from each species and on all *D. cinerosa* gut samples to identify current bacterial symbionts (Wood et al. 2019). In order to test whether tissue-level expression is correlated with chromosome-specific paralog duplication, *Disholcaspis cinerosa* target genes were matched to *B. kinseyi* paralogs using gene tree topology. Transcriptome sequences were assigned to CynOG by hmmsearch. Cynipoid reads were quantified against genome-derived transcript sets or Trinity assemblies with Salmon v1.10.3 (Patro et al. 2017). Expression values are given as transcripts per million (TPM), normalized to the total transcripts per sample.

## Target gene identification

While the three PCWDE genes had previously been identified by gene name, more precise enzyme family assignments were needed to identify target chemistry and potential bacterial donors. All predicted proteins were scanned against the dbCAN HMM database (dbCAN-HMMdb-V10; Yin et al 2012\) at the subfamily level (dbCAN-sub v5.2.4; Zheng et al 2023\) using HMMER3.4 hmmsearch (E-value \< 10^-10) to assign CAZy classifications and domain boundaries (Cantarel et al. 2009). Subfamily assignments in each cynipoid PCWDE family were validated by submitting a representative structural pdb from each family to the CAZyme3D database (Shanmugam and Yin, 2025). Structures were predicted with LocalColabFold v1.5.2 using the AlphaFold2-ptm model (Jumper et al. 2021; Mirdita et al. 2022), one model per sequence, three recycles, ranking by pLDDT, and MMseqs2 UniRef/environmental MSAs (--model-type alphafold2\_ptm \--num-models 1 \--num-recycle 3 \--rank plddt \--msa-mode mmseqs2\_uniref\_env; Steinegger and Soding, 2017). Signal peptides were predicted with SignalP v6 and removed before folding where present (Teufel et al. 2022). Full CAZy results were retained, including substrates. After enzyme type information allowed us to predict specific oligosaccharides generated by each PCWDE, we searched the dbCAN-sub listed substrates of other CAZy hits to identify enzymes potentially capable of degrading oligosaccharides further. 

During preliminary search, we identified a fourth *B. kinseyi* gene of apparently bacterial origin: a compact B-lectin carbohydrate-binding module. dbCAN HMM search was inadequate to identify all members of this gene family because domain intactness varied and many eukaryotic B-lectin genes exist. To identify only genes of the apparently bacterial B-lectin, we used the membership in the CynOG containing the *B. kinseyi* gene, including assigned transcripts, then validated that they contained at least the B-lectin domain. For each protein, full domain boundaries were assigned using HMMER3 hmmscan against the Pfam-A database (Mistry et al. 2021\) at conditional E-value \<1e-3. Using the native *B kinseyi* assembly from NCBI or Ragtag or JCVI *B. kinseyi* chromosome assignments, each target hit in any family was assigned to one of the 10 *B. kinseyi* chromosomes, or marked as unplaced if found on an unassigned scaffold.

Some genomes may contain evidence of bacterial genes too degraded to recover in annotation. To detect these pseudogene fragments, a representative sequence for each gene family was used as a tblastn v2.16.0+ (E-value \< 1e-5, SEG filtering enabled, max 200 targets; Camacho et al. 2009\) query against all 75 assemblies, including retained, alternate, and excluded assemblies. To avoid false positives, tblastn hits were treated as provisional and results were evaluated only for genome-gene family combinations lacking an accepted Helixer/dbCAN family member. For GH5\_2, PL1\_8, and PL4\_4, candidate HSPs were required to overlap the focal query domain by at least 150, 150, and 250 amino acids, respectively, and to have at least 45% amino-acid identity. B-lectin candidates were required to derive from a non-plant query, align for at least 90 amino acids, have a bit score of at least 120, and have at least 40% identity. Finally, HSP findings were queried against CynOGs and hits that didn’t match the corresponding PCWDE gene were removed. 

## Outgroup sequences

To identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome, outgroup sequences were identified using three methods. First, representative *B. kinseyi* protein sequences from each gene family were used as DIAMOND BLASTp (Buchfink et al. 2021\) queries against the NCBI nr database (E value \<10^-10, query coverage \>50%, up to 1000 hits per query). Where possible, corresponding CDS were found in the nt database by matching accession number or using tblastn. Second, after CAZy subfamily assignments for each gene family were determined, all listed members of those subfamilies were identified on [CAZy.org](http://CAZy.org) and downloaded from NCBI using GenBank accession numbers. Finally, an additional set of outgroup matches were found by searching structural prediction pdb files for a single representative gene from the cynipoid set against the CAZyme3D database. 

## Gene Phylogenies

Because input sequence composition is heterogeneous due to accessory domains and gene model errors, we repredicted dbCAN subfamily domains at a more permissive E-value (\<10^-5) and trimmed sequences to the dbCAN hmmsearch-predicted target domain plus 50 flanking amino acids. Proteins containing multiple non-overlapping domain hits covering over 30% of the HMM profile were split into independent units. Apparent gene-prediction fusions were excluded only when the protein contained a single partial target domain in an otherwise unrelated ORF, were \>2x the family median length, and their target domain covered \<50% of the protein.

Two sets of protein trees were created for each gene family: transfer-context trees inferred from the complete accepted cynipoid-plus-outgroup sets (427 GH5\_2, 365 PL1\_8, and 446 PL4\_4 sequences), and cynipoid locus trees rooted on a bacterial outgroup to resolve post-transfer duplication history. For display, Figure 2 was pruned to 30 tips per family. All outgroup tips and three to four cynipoid representatives appear in Figure S1. Pruning did not alter the inferred topology, branch lengths, or support values. To generate these phylogenies, sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Maximum-likelihood protein trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (Wong et al. 2026\) under a fixed LG+G4 model. Branch support was estimated with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates (-B 1000; Hoang et al. 2018). For B-lectin domain proteins, because many contained more than one copy of the domain, tree building was conducted in two stages. First, all B-lectin domains were isolated and a tree was constructed as above (Figure S4). In that tree, each domain was assigned to either a branch containing the N-terminal or C-terminal outgroup B-lectin. Subsequently, these two branches were aligned as partitions and analyzed in IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under an edge-proportional linked model (-p). ModelFinder (-m MFP; Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017\) selected HIVB+G4 for D1 and WAG+G4 for D2. Branch support was assessed with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates with BNNI (-B 1000 \-bnni; Hoang et al. 2018\) and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates (-alrt 1000; Guindon et al. 2010). 

## Intron analysis

Intron presence and position were analyzed as evidence of integration into wasp genomes. Consistently placed introns are strong evidence that a gene is incorporated into the cynipoid genome, not spurious hits from unfiltered bacterial reads. The phylogenetic conservation of intron placement also provides an independent line of evidence to infer when each transfer occurred. Intron positions were extracted from gene model predictions for each target hit in each genomic reference in which they were found. Introns were defined as gaps between consecutive CDS features. For each family, a single *B. kinseyi* reference protein was selected. All other genomic homologs were aligned to that reference with Biopython's PairwiseAligner (BLOSUM62, gap open −10, gap extend −0.5, global mode; Cock et al. 2009). Each intron was assigned an amino acid position relative to the coordinates of that reference sequence. Intron placements were clustered at a maximum distance of three amino acids. For each intron placement cluster, the dominant splice phase, splice signal, and chromosomal distribution of genes with that intron site were recorded. Flanking context was extracted for each modal site to verify that introns were found at consistent placements within ungapped, unambiguous alignment regions. 

## Catalytic residue identification and selection analysis

After insertion into eukaryotic genomes, bacterial genes are exposed to new selection pressures and may experience losses of catalytic capacity, altered binding site geometry, or changed surface chemistry. To confirm that each protein retained the experimentally determined catalytic residues for its family, sequence alignment was used to identify catalytic amino acid positions. Catalytic residues were defined from experimentally characterized family members. GH5\_2 positions were referenced to *Bacillus agaradherans* Cel5A (PDB 1A3H, chain A), in which E139 is the catalytic acid/base and E228 is the nucleophile (Davies et al. 1998). The conserved PL1 catalytic arginine was referenced to R236 of *Aspergillus niger* pectin lyase B (PDB 1QCX, chain A; Vitali et al. 1998), corresponding to the homologous R236 experimentally demonstrated to be essential for catalysis in *A. niger* pectin lyase A (Sánchez-Torres et al. 2003). PL4\_4 positions were referenced to K150 and H210 of *Aspergillus aculeatus* rhamnogalacturonan lyase (PDB 1NKG, chain A; McDonough et al. 2004), which are proposed to act as the proton abstractor and proton donor, respectively (Jensen et al. 2010). Reference sequences and the locked cynipoid domain sequences were aligned de novo with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 1). Each mapped position was classified as containing the expected reference residue, a substitution, or a gap/ambiguous residue. Gaps and ambiguous positions were reported separately and excluded from percentages calculated among observable sites (Table S7). 

To generate codon alignments for selection analyses, we retrieved the coding sequence corresponding to each retained cynipoid protein. A coding sequence was included only when its translation exactly reproduced the protein sequence used in the family alignment. Protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 8), and coding sequences were threaded onto the protein alignments. Codon columns containing gaps in more than 50% of sequences were removed. Maximum-likelihood trees were inferred from the filtered codon alignments with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under GTR+G4 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000 \-T 16 \--safe; Hoang et al. 2018).

To identify branches with evidence of episodic diversifying selection within Cynipoidea, we used aBSREL (Smith et al. 2015\) in HyPhy v2.5.85 (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020\) with \--branches All. Each branch was tested independently for a proportion of sites with omega greater than 1, and significance was assessed at the HyPhy Holm-corrected p \< 0.05 threshold.

Because bacterial outgroups are too divergent for reliable codon-level comparison, per-site selection analysis was restricted to cynipoid sequences. FUBAR (Murrell et al. 2013\) was run in HyPhy v2.5.85 with default parameters. Sites were classified as under purifying or diversifying selection at posterior probability \> 0.9; all other retained sites were classified as neutral for the summary.
