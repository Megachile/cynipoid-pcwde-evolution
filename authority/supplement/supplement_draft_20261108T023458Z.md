## S1. A chromosome-scale genome for *Euceroptres primus*

\[Author text: section context\]

We generated a chromosomal-scale genome of *Euceroptres primus* following the protocol outlined in Zhang et al (2026). Briefly, the samples were reared from 53 galls on *Quercus bicolor* in Pennsylvania, 2025\. A single female wasp was homogenized and used for high-molecular-weight DNA extraction, sheared to \~18 Kb, and prepared into a PacBio SMRTbell library with barcoded adapters, then sequenced on a PacBio Revio system; concurrently, a pool of three females was used for to prepare a Hi-C library using the enzymes DdeI and DpnII and was subsequently sequenced on an Element AVITI system. HiFi reads were filtered using FCS-Adaptor and HiFiAdapterFilt (Sim et al. 2022), assembled with HiFiASM (Cheng et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022\) using the insect telomere motif (Vitkova et al. 2005), purged of duplicates with PurgeDups (Guan et al. 2020), and scaffolded with Hi-C reads via BWAmem2 (Vasimuddin et al. 2019), Picard (Broad Institute 2019), and YaHS (Zhou et al. 2023), with manual editing in Juicebox (Durand et al. 2016); contig coverage was assessed with Minimap2 (Li 2018), completeness with BUSCO (Tegenfeldt et al. 2025\) using hymenoptera\_odb12, and taxonomic assignments with BLAST+ (Camacho et al. 2009), Diamond (Buchfink et al. 2021), and FCS-GX (Astashyn et al. 2024), summarized with Blobtools2 (Challis et al. 2020\) and blobblurb, to remove non-arthropod and Wolbachia contigs. A transcriptome from a single female was sequenced, with reads filtered by Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014\) and expression quantified with Salmon (Patro et al. 2017). Genome annotation was performed using EGAPx with STAR, Miniprot, and Gnomon, supplemented by BRAKER3 (Gabriel et al. 2024\) with RepeatModeler and OrthoDB v12, while genomic elements were derived with bedtools (Quinlan and Hall 2010\) and Pfam domains assigned via HMMER (Eddy 2011; Mistry et al. 2021).

A new chromosome-scale genome assembly was generated for the figitid species *Euceroptres primus.* Preliminary work showed that this was the only sampled figitid to retain PCWDEs. Confident resolution of the chromosomal context of each gene in this species provides important evidence about the timing and placement of the original lateral transfer events.

PacBio HiFi sequencing yielded a highly contiguous genome for *Euceroptres primus*. From one Revio cell where the barcoded *E. primus* SMRTBell library represented one third of the sequencing run, 2,706,303 HiFi reads (37.95 Gb of HiFi data) were obtained, of which 30 (0.0011% of reads) were discarded after filtering with HiFiAdapterFilt. K-mer analysis of the final assembly relative to the PacBio HiFi reads used to create the contig assemblies reported raw and QV scores of 65.867 and 63.392 respectively. The genome coverage was estimated at 44.6× coverage as reported by GenomeScope2.

The final genome consisted of 15 scaffolds totaling 839 MB, an N50 of 155 MB, L50 of 3, N90 of 107 MB, and L90 of 6 (Figure S1). The assembly has a fairly complete hymenoptera\_odb12 BUSCO, C:97.4% \[S: 96.5%, D:0.9%\], F:0.8%, M:1.8% (n \= 5,920). Hi-C scaffolding revealed six chromosomes (Figure S1) with only 8 contigs representing 970 Kb (0.12%) of the genome remaining unplaced, and the genome was mostly homozygous. EGAPx annotated 14,633 genes, including 12,973 protein-coding genes and 1,737 lncRNA.

The majority of prior evidence from genomes assembled to chromosome scale (*T. remus,* *Dr. kuriphilus, B. kinseyi;* Xu et al 2021, Liu et al 2024\) and from direct karyotyping (Gokhman et al 2011, 2015\) have found that the modal chromosome count in cynipids is n=10, which is likely ancestral among Platygastroidea and Cynipoidea (Gokhman 2009). Only a single figitid species, *Callaspidia defonscolombei,* has been documented with more than 10 (n=11, Gokhman et al 2011). To assign comparable spatial coordinates despite varying chromosome number, we used RagTag to align all non-chromosome scale genomes to the 10 chromosome-scale scaffolds of the GCF\_010883055.1 *B. kinseyi* assembly, numbered by length in decreasing order, as reference (Alonge 2022). For *Euceroptres primus,* which was assembled to 6 chromosome-scale scaffolds, JCVI v1.5.11 was used to assign target genes to *B. kinseyi*\-chromosome-equivalent regions (Tang et al. 2024). Because reference-guided scaffolding can join fragments across chromosomal boundaries, subsequent references to chromosomes 1-10 refer to genomic blocks based on the 10 *B. kinseyi* pseudochromosomes and are used as a coordinate system rather than statements about karyotype.

Since only three of the genomes (*B. kinseyi*, *Leptopilina boulardi* and *L. heterotoma*) were previously annotated, Helixer v0.3.6 (model invertebrate\_v0.3\_m\_0100; Stiehler et al. 2021\) was used to predict genomic elements. NCBI annotations were retained only for *B. kinseyi*.

## S3. Outgroup sequences found in the screens

\[Author text: section context\]

Outgroup sequences were screened in the NCBI nr, CAZy, and CAZyme3D databases to identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome. After deduplication, our outgroup screens identified 308 total PCWDE outgroup sequences (Table S3). For PL1\_8, we found 57 bacterial sequences and 6 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (3 Cecidomyiidae, 2 Apidae, 1 Oomycota). For PL4\_4, we found 65 bacterial sequences and 14 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (6 fungus, 4 *Cuscuta*, 4 Coleoptera). For GH5\_2, we found 154 bacterial sequences and 12 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (9 Nematoda, 2 Protista, 1 Coleoptera). Because we discovered an additional putatively bacterial domain in the full B-lectin gene family, outgroup screens were conducted separately for PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3 and B-lectin. We found 35 bacterial PI-PLC-X sequences. For GH30\_3, we found 122 bacteria and 66 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (40 fungus, 20 metazoa, 5 rotifer, 1 plant). For B-lectin, we found 112 bacteria and 158 non-cynipoid eukaryote sequences (66 plant, 38 fungus, 36 metazoa, 18 rotifer). However, only one outgroup sequence contained the full intact domain architecture including PI-PLC-X, GH30\_3, and tandem B-lectin: bacterial species *Actinoplanes awajinensis*.

Other eukaryotic sequences (e.g., Nematoda, Cecidomyiidae, Apidae, Coleoptera) identified in our outgroup screen are outside the scope of this study, but many represent potential cases of lateral transfer from bacteria not previously documented in the literature. Of particular note is that PL1\_8 was acquired independently by members of two phylogenetically distant galling lineages: the cecidomyiid gall midges (Diptera) and the cynipoid gall wasps (Hymenoptera).

While the three PCWDE genes had previously been identified by gene name, more precise enzyme family assignments were needed to identify target chemistry and potential bacterial donors. All predicted proteins were scanned against the dbCAN HMM database (dbCAN-HMMdb-V10; Yin et al 2012\) at the subfamily level (dbCAN-sub v5.2.4; Zheng et al 2023\) using HMMER3.4 hmmsearch (E-value \< 10^-10) to assign CAZy classifications and domain boundaries (Cantarel et al. 2009). Subfamily assignments in each cynipoid PCWDE family were validated by submitting a representative structural pdb from each family to the CAZyme3D database (Siva Shanmugam and Yin, 2025). Structures were predicted with LocalColabFold v1.5.2 using the AlphaFold2-ptm model (Jumper et al. 2021; Mirdita et al. 2022), one model per sequence, three recycles, ranking by pLDDT, and MMseqs2 UniRef/environmental MSAs (--model-type alphafold2\_ptm \--num-models 1 \--num-recycle 3 \--rank plddt \--msa-mode mmseqs2\_uniref\_env; Steinegger and Soding, 2017). Signal peptides were predicted with SignalP v6 and removed before folding where present (Teufel et al. 2022). Full CAZy results were retained, including substrates. After enzyme type information allowed us to predict specific oligosaccharides generated by each PCWDE, we searched the dbCAN-sub listed substrates of other CAZy hits to identify enzymes potentially capable of degrading oligosaccharides further.

During preliminary search, we identified a fourth *B. kinseyi* gene of apparently bacterial origin: a compact B-lectin carbohydrate-binding module. dbCAN HMM search was inadequate to identify all members of this gene family because domain intactness varied and many eukaryotic B-lectin genes exist. To identify only genes of the apparently bacterial B-lectin, we used the membership in the CynOG containing the *B. kinseyi* gene, including assigned transcripts, then validated that they contained at least the B-lectin domain. For each protein, full domain boundaries were assigned using HMMER3 hmmscan against the Pfam-A database (Mistry et al. 2021\) at conditional E-value \<1e-3. Using the native *B kinseyi* assembly from NCBI or Ragtag or JCVI *B. kinseyi* chromosome assignments, each target hit in any family was assigned to one of the 10 *B. kinseyi* chromosomes, or marked as unplaced if found on an unassigned scaffold.

Some genomes may contain evidence of bacterial genes too degraded to recover in annotation. To detect these pseudogene fragments, a representative sequence for each gene family was used as a tblastn v2.16.0+ (E-value \< 1e-5, SEG filtering enabled, max 200 targets; Camacho et al. 2009\) query against all 75 assemblies, including retained, alternate, and excluded assemblies. To avoid false positives, tblastn hits were treated as provisional and results were evaluated only for genome-gene family combinations lacking an accepted Helixer/dbCAN family member. For GH5\_2, PL1\_8, and PL4\_4, candidate HSPs were required to overlap the focal query domain by at least 150, 150, and 250 amino acids, respectively, and to have at least 45% amino-acid identity. B-lectin candidates were required to derive from a non-plant query, align for at least 90 amino acids, have a bit score of at least 120, and have at least 40% identity. Finally, HSP findings were queried against CynOGs and hits that didn’t match the corresponding PCWDE gene were removed. The *Parnips nigripes* PL1\_8-like region, whose tBLASTn hits fell below the 45% identity threshold, was characterized separately by protein-to-genome alignment of two cynipid PL1\_8 proteins (*Aulacidea tavakolii* and *Ceroptres masudai*) with miniprot v0.9 and exonerate v2.4.0 (protein2genome), followed by a dbCAN-sub hmmsearch of the translated alignment.

To identify potential bacterial donors and assess sequence and structural changes after being incorporated into a eukaryotic genome, outgroup sequences were identified using three methods. First, representative *B. kinseyi* protein sequences from each gene family were used as DIAMOND BLASTp (Buchfink et al. 2021\) queries against the NCBI nr database (E value \<10^-10, query coverage \>50%, up to 1000 hits per query). Where possible, corresponding CDS were found in the nt database by matching accession number or using tblastn. Second, after CAZy subfamily assignments for each gene family were determined, all listed members of those subfamilies were identified on [CAZy.org](http://cazy.org) and downloaded from NCBI using GenBank accession numbers. Finally, an additional set of outgroup matches were found by searching structural prediction pdb files for a single representative gene from the cynipoid set against the CAZyme3D database.

## S4. Gene-tree construction

\[Author text: section context\]

Because input sequence composition is heterogeneous due to accessory domains and gene model errors, we repredicted dbCAN subfamily domains at a more permissive E-value (\<10^-5) and trimmed sequences to the dbCAN hmmsearch-predicted target domain plus 50 flanking amino acids. Proteins containing multiple non-overlapping domain hits covering over 30% of the HMM profile were split into independent units. Where dbCAN hits for the same family overlapped on one protein (including hits sharing only an endpoint), only the hit with the lowest E-value was kept. Apparent gene-prediction fusions were excluded only when the protein contained a single partial target domain in an otherwise unrelated ORF, were \>2x the family median length, and their target domain covered \<50% of the protein.

To build alignments for phylogenetic trees, cynipoid and outgroup protein sequences were filtered to remove incomplete or unusually long gene predictions. Two sets of protein trees were created for each gene family: transfer-context trees inferred from the complete accepted cynipoid-plus-outgroup sets (427 GH5\_2, 364 PL1\_8, and 444 PL4\_4 sequences), and cynipoid locus trees rooted on a bacterial outgroup to resolve post-transfer duplication history. Maximum-likelihood protein trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (Wong et al. 2026\) under a fixed LG+G4 model. Branch support was estimated with 1,000 UFBoot2 replicates (-B 1000; Hoang et al. 2018). Each family's tree was rooted on the bacterial sequence with the smallest mean patristic distance to the cynipoid tips in the transfer-context tree. The final trees contain 255 GH5\_2, 294 PL1\_8 and 358 PL4\_4 cynipoid tips plus that outgroup. Four *Bassettia pallida* transcript-derived sequences (one PL1\_8, two PL4\_4 and one B-lectin) were 99.1–100% identical in aligned protein sequence to *Callirhytis quercusbatatoides* sequences and lay 0.000–0.014 substitutions/site from them, so they were attributed to *C. quercusbatatoides*. Where a strongly supported locus-tree grouping conflicted with the species tree (Figure 1), we tested whether each gene's own data rejected the species-tree arrangement (Table S4). For each conflict, a maximum-likelihood tree was re-inferred with only the focal species constrained to the species-tree grouping (IQ-TREE v3.0.1, LG+G4), and the unconstrained and constrained trees were compared with the approximately unbiased (AU) test (10,000 RELL replicates; Shimodaira 2002). Site concordance factors (100 sampled quartets; Minh et al. 2020\) were calculated for the conflicting and constrained branches. To check for long-branch effects, trees were rebuilt without terminal branches of 0.6 or more substitutions/site and compared on the remaining tips. None of the 17 tests rejected the species-tree arrangement (all p-AU ≥ 0.09, without multiple-testing correction), and all nine conflicts tested for long-branch effects persisted after long-branch removal.

## S6. Ortholog catalog and synteny blocks

\[Author text: section context\]

To identify orthologous loci within each family, we compared flanking gene sequences adjacent to each occurrence of each gene. This analysis also allowed us to validate that genes for each target family are integrated into cynipoid genomes and rule out bacterial sequences included due to assembly errors. Synteny detection was complicated by the recurrence of repetitive Transposable Elements (TEs) among predicted gene elements. To mitigate that problem, RepeatModeler v2.0.7 was used to create a per-genome library of repetitive elements (Flynn et al. 2020). This library was deduplicated, filtered to remove known genes, and used to guide RepeatMasker v4.2.2 softmasking of all genomes (Smit et al. 2013-2015). The final 49-genome panel contained 920,372 predicted proteins. An orthology catalog was initially constructed from 667,137 proteins from 41 genome proteomes after excluding proteins for which at least 30% of CDS bases were repeat-masked. Proteins were assigned to orthogroups, designated CynOGs, with OrthoFinder v2.5.5 in orthogroups-only mode (-og), using DIAMOND for sequence search (-S diamond; Buchfink et al. 2021), 32 search and analysis threads (-t 32 \-a 32), and MCL inflation 1.5 (van Dongen 2000). After merging co-linear gene-model fragments, proteins with at least 15% repeat overlap were removed from orthogroups represented in five or fewer genomes; orthogroups left without members were discarded. This produced 22,448 CynOGs containing 623,796 proteins.

Profile HMMs were constructed for CynOGs containing at least three members. Sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto; Katoh and Standley 2013), trimmed with trimAl (-automated1; Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009), and converted to profile HMMs with HMMER 3.4 hmmbuild (Eddy 2011), yielding 17,928 profiles. A self-recovery check using hmmsearch assigned 99 of 100 randomly selected source proteins to the correct CynOG, and the profile library was indexed with hmmpress. Proteins from nine later-added or replacement proteomes and proteins predicted from transcriptomes were searched against this library at E \< 1 × 10⁻⁵ and assigned to the CynOG with the best full-sequence E-value and score.

Because genome size and intergenic distance vary substantially within Cynipoidea, synteny was evaluated using gene order rather than physical distance. For each genome, we therefore created an index listing CynOG assignments in their order of appearance along each pseudochromosome. To verify homology between paralogs, flanking gene context was used to define synteny blocks. Synteny blocks were identified by counting flanking genes at windows of 5, 15, and 50 on either side of each target gene, excluding tandem duplicates of the target itself. Pairwise synteny between two loci of a given family was assessed using the Jaccard index of their flanking CynOG signatures. Locus pairs with Jaccard ≥0.15 at any window size were considered syntenic. Synteny blocks were identified by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on the narrow-window (±5 genes) Jaccard distance matrix (1 − J), cutting the dendrogram at distance 0.85 (corresponding to J \= 0.15). Because this threshold requires a relatively small number of shared genes, it is common for loci to be assigned connections to multiple neighbors that themselves share no flanking genes. This transitive chaining can link wholly unrelated genome regions as false synteny blocks. Average linkage mitigates this problem by requiring each potential match to resemble all members of an existing block. Confidence tiers of synteny blocks were assigned based on which window sizes supported the pair: high (detected at ±5 genes), medium (±15 genes only), or low (±50 genes only).

After shared synteny blocks were defined, we looked for each conserved flanking gene pattern across all 49 genomes. We filtered the flanking gene set to only those present in more than 50% of block members and looked for occurrences clustered within 500 kb. After a synteny block was identified, we rescanned all gene models within the search region for target hits. When a match was discovered, the genome contained a target hit at that locus, and the number of tandem repeats was counted; if it didn’t, the locus was marked EMPTY. If fewer than 3 conserved flanking genes could be found in a cluster, the block was marked NOT\_DETERMINED for that genome.

To identify true orthologous loci within each family and to verify the absence of genes in lineages where the target proteins weren’t found, we compared flanking gene sequences adjacent to each occurrence of each gene to identify blocks of conserved synteny. A total of 84 flanking gene patterns could be matched across more than one genome \[CHECK \#10: Synteny update 448/675 awaits step B: pending until synteny step B merges\] (Table S6). Of these, 27 contained target genes in two or more genomes, accounting for 79.8% (455/570) of genome-derived target loci. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 10 was recovered in 37/49 genomes; 26 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 11 were empty. The modal GH5\_2 block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 44/49 genomes; 22 contained at least one GH5\_2 gene and 22 were empty. The modal PL1\_8 block on chromosome 8 was recovered in 42/49 genomes, including 27 with PL1\_8 and 15 empty. The modal PL4\_4 block on chromosome 7 was recovered in 36/49 genomes, including 28 with PL4\_4 and eight empty. The modal B-lectin block on chromosome 6 was recovered in 40/49 genomes, including 13 with B-lectin and 27 empty.

Target genes in many synteny loci occur in tandem duplications, up to a maximum of 10 per locus. In many cases, synteny blocks recovered by our criteria as independent are found in relatively close proximity; six PL1\_8 blocks were separated by fewer than 20 genes. Synteny blocks were also detected in species lacking target genes, marked as absent in Table S6, giving strong evidence that absence of the gene in those species indicates a block predates insertion or postdates loss, rather than poor assembly quality. Species taxonomically closer to *B. kinseyi* recovered more flanking genes per locus and synteny blocks were less likely to be undetectable even in poor quality assemblies. In the most distant species, the outgroup Platygastroid *T. remus*, only 24/77 cross-genome-detected synteny blocks were recovered, all of which lacked the target protein, supporting the inference that the transfer happened after the divergence of Cynipoidea and Platygastroidea.

## S9. GC3 composition of transferred genes

\[Author text: section context\]

*\[Moved verbatim from the main-text Methods (draft of 2026-11-03)\]*

Intron presence and position were analyzed as evidence of integration into wasp genomes. Consistently placed introns are strong evidence that a gene is incorporated into the cynipoid genome, not spurious hits from unfiltered bacterial reads. The phylogenetic conservation of intron placement also provides an independent line of evidence to infer when each transfer occurred. Intron positions were extracted from gene model predictions for each target hit in each genomic reference in which they were found. Introns were defined as gaps between consecutive CDS features. For each family, a single *B. kinseyi* reference protein was selected. All other genomic homologs were aligned to that reference with Biopython's PairwiseAligner (BLOSUM62, gap open −10, gap extend −0.5, global mode; Cock et al. 2009). Each intron was assigned an amino acid position relative to the coordinates of that reference sequence. Intron placements were clustered at a maximum distance of three amino acids. For each intron placement cluster, the dominant splice phase, splice signal, and chromosomal distribution of genes with that intron site were recorded. Flanking context was extracted for each modal site to verify that introns were found at consistent placements within ungapped, unambiguous alignment regions.

## S10. Do catalytic-residue substitutions cluster in the gene trees?

\[Author text: section context; methods and results\]

*\[Moved verbatim from the main-text Methods (draft of 2026-11-03)\]*

After insertion into eukaryotic genomes, bacterial genes are exposed to new selection pressures and may experience losses of catalytic capacity, altered binding site geometry, or changed surface chemistry. To confirm that each protein retained the experimentally determined catalytic residues for its family, sequence alignment was used to identify catalytic amino acid positions. Catalytic residues were defined from experimentally characterized family members. GH5\_2 positions were referenced to *Bacillus agaradherans* Cel5A (PDB 1A3H, chain A), in which E139 is the catalytic acid/base and E228 is the nucleophile (Davies et al. 1998). The conserved PL1 catalytic arginine was referenced to R236 of *Aspergillus niger* pectin lyase B (PDB 1QCX, chain A; Vitali et al. 1998), corresponding to the homologous R236 experimentally demonstrated to be essential for catalysis in *A. niger* pectin lyase A (Sánchez-Torres et al. 2003). PL4\_4 positions were referenced to K150 and H210 of *Aspergillus aculeatus* rhamnogalacturonan lyase (PDB 1NKG, chain A; McDonough et al. 2004), which are proposed to act as the proton abstractor and proton donor, respectively (Jensen et al. 2010). Reference sequences and the locked cynipoid domain sequences were aligned de novo with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 1). Each mapped position was classified as containing the expected reference residue, a substitution, or a gap/ambiguous residue. Gaps and ambiguous positions were reported separately and excluded from percentages calculated among observable sites (Table S10). To test whether catalytic-residue substitutions were concentrated in particular clades, we counted the minimum number of changes between expected and substituted residues on the transfer-context trees (Fitch parsimony) and compared it with 10,000 permutations of residue states among tips, preserving each tip’s set of callable sites; P is the proportion of permutations with as few or fewer changes than observed (Table S11).

To generate codon alignments for selection analyses, we retrieved the coding sequence corresponding to each retained cynipoid protein. A coding sequence was included only when its translation exactly reproduced the protein sequence used in the family alignment. Protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto \--thread 8), and coding sequences were threaded onto the protein alignments. Codon columns containing gaps in more than 50% of sequences were removed. Maximum-likelihood trees were inferred from the filtered codon alignments with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 under GTR+G4 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000 \-T 16 \--safe; Hoang et al. 2018).

To identify branches with evidence of episodic diversifying selection within Cynipoidea, we used aBSREL (Smith et al. 2015\) in HyPhy v2.5.85 (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020\) with \--branches All. Each branch was tested independently for a proportion of sites with omega greater than 1, and significance was assessed at the HyPhy Holm-corrected p \< 0.05 threshold.

Because bacterial outgroups are too divergent for reliable codon-level comparison, per-site selection analysis was restricted to cynipoid sequences. FUBAR (Murrell et al. 2013\) was run in HyPhy v2.5.85 with default parameters. Sites were classified as under purifying or diversifying selection at posterior probability \> 0.9; all other retained sites were classified as neutral for the summary.
